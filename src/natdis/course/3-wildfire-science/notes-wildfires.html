Unit 3: Wildfire Science - Lecture Notes

Overview

This unit explores how communities can adapt to coexist with the increasing threat of wildfire as the climate changes. We examine the complex interplay of climate, ecology, and human development at the wildland-urban interface, using satellite data and case studies to understand wildfire dynamics, design fire-resilient communities, and develop effective land use policies.

Part 1: The Scientist’s Perspective

1.1 The Fire Triangle

Key Concepts

  • Fire Triangle: The three elements required for fire to exist: fuel, oxygen, and heat
  • Combustion: The chemical reaction that occurs when fuel, oxygen, and heat combine
  • Fire Behavior: How a fire ignites, spreads, and develops based on environmental conditions
  • Fire Ecology: The study of the relationship between fire and the ecosystem

Elements of the Fire Triangle

Fuel

  • Any combustible material that can burn
  • Includes vegetation (grass, shrubs, trees), structures, and other flammable materials
  • Key characteristics:
    • Fuel moisture content: Higher moisture = harder to ignite
    • Fuel load: Amount of available fuel per unit area
    • Fuel arrangement: How fuels are distributed horizontally and vertically
    • Fuel continuity: Whether fuels are continuous or separated by gaps

Oxygen

  • Present in the atmosphere (approximately 21%)
  • Wind affects oxygen availability by:
    • Bringing fresh oxygen to the fire
    • Removing combustion products
    • Pre-heating fuels ahead of the fire front
    • Tilting flames toward unburned fuel

Heat

  • Required for ignition and continued combustion
  • Sources include:
    • Lightning
    • Human activities (campfires, discarded cigarettes, power lines)
    • Heat from existing fires
    • Radiant heat transfer
    • Convective heat transfer

Fire Science Fundamentals

Ignition Phase

  • Pre-heating of fuel
  • Moisture evaporation
  • Release of volatile gases
  • Ignition temperature: ~300°C (570°F) for many forest fuels

Combustion Phase

  • Flaming combustion: Rapid oxidation producing visible flames
  • Smoldering combustion: Slower, flameless burning of char
  • Complete vs. incomplete combustion

Heat Transfer Mechanisms

  • Radiation: Transfer of heat through electromagnetic waves
  • Conduction: Direct transfer of heat through physical contact
  • Convection: Transfer of heat through the movement of air or liquids

1.2 Weather, Climate, and Topography

Weather Factors Affecting Wildfires

Temperature

  • Higher temperatures accelerate moisture evaporation from fuels
  • Increases likelihood of ignition and rate of spread
  • Affects firefighter safety and operational effectiveness

Humidity

  • Low relative humidity increases fire risk by drying fuels
  • Critical threshold: Below 30% relative humidity significantly increases fire danger
  • Daily humidity cycles affect fire behavior (typically lower in afternoon)

Wind

  • Primary factor in fire spread rate and direction
  • Increases oxygen supply to the fire
  • Influences flame length and intensity
  • Creates spot fires by carrying embers ahead of the main fire
  • Formula: $Rate \, of \, Spread = Base \, Rate \times Wind \, Factor$

Precipitation

  • Affects fuel moisture content directly
  • Long-term drought increases fire potential
  • Short-term drought (seasonal) affects fine fuels

Climate Change and Fire Regimes

Shifting Fire Seasons

  • Longer fire seasons in many regions
  • Earlier spring snowmelt in mountain areas
  • Increased frequency of extreme fire weather days

Drought Patterns

  • Increased duration and intensity of droughts
  • Greater moisture stress on vegetation
  • Conversion of live fuels to dead fuels

Vegetation Changes

  • Shifts in plant communities due to changing climate conditions
  • Potential for invasive species that may be more fire-prone
  • Tree mortality from insects and disease exacerbated by climate stress

Topographic Influences

Slope

  • Fire spreads faster uphill
  • Rule of thumb: Rate of spread doubles for each 10° increase in slope
  • Heat pre-conditioning of upslope fuels through radiation and convection
  • Formula: $Slope \, Effect = Base \, Rate \times 2^{(Slope \, in \, degrees/10)}$

Aspect

  • South and west-facing slopes receive more solar radiation
  • North-facing slopes tend to be cooler and moister
  • Southeast to southwest aspects typically have highest fire danger

Elevation

  • Affects temperature, precipitation, and vegetation types
  • Higher elevations often have shorter fire seasons
  • Valley bottoms can channel winds (Venturi effect)

Terrain Features

  • Ridges, valleys, and canyons affect wind patterns
  • Narrow canyons can create chimney effects, accelerating fire
  • Saddles and gaps concentrate wind flow

1.3 Fire Behavior and Analysis

Fire Behavior Metrics

Rate of Spread

  • Speed at which a fire moves across the landscape
  • Usually measured in chains per hour (1 chain = 66 feet) or meters per minute
  • Formula: $Rate \, of \, Spread = \frac{Distance}{Time}$

Flame Length

  • Distance from the base of the flame to its tip
  • Indicator of fire intensity and potential for crown fire
  • Determines appropriate suppression tactics

Fire Intensity

  • Heat energy released per unit time per unit length of fire front
  • Measured in kilowatts per meter (kW/m)
  • Formula: $Intensity = Heat \, of \, Combustion \times Fuel \, Load \times Rate \, of \, Spread$

Spotting Distance

  • Distance that firebrands (embers) can travel ahead of the main fire
  • Major factor in structure ignitions at the wildland-urban interface
  • Can range from meters to kilometers depending on conditions

Fire Types and Classification

Ground Fires

  • Burns organic matter beneath surface litter
  • Typically slow-moving but difficult to detect and extinguish
  • Common in areas with deep organic soils or heavy duff layers

Surface Fires

  • Burns surface litter, debris, and small vegetation
  • Most common wildfire type
  • Fire intensity varies widely based on fuel and weather

Crown Fires

  • Burns through the canopy of trees
  • Highest intensity and most difficult to control
  • Types:
    • Passive crown fire: Individual tree or group torching
    • Active crown fire: Fire advances through crowns independent of surface fire
    • Independent crown fire: Rare, advances through crowns without surface fire

Prescribed Fires

  • Intentionally set under controlled conditions
  • Used for fuel reduction, habitat improvement, or restoration
  • Requires detailed planning and favorable weather conditions

Remote Sensing and Fire Detection

Satellite Technology

  • MODIS (Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer)
  • VIIRS (Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite)
  • Landsat for pre- and post-fire analysis
  • Detection of thermal anomalies and active fire fronts

Fire Danger Rating Systems

  • National Fire Danger Rating System (NFDRS)
  • Canadian Forest Fire Danger Rating System
  • Components include:
    • Fuel moisture codes
    • Fire weather indices
    • Spread components
    • Energy release components

Fire Behavior Prediction Tools

  • BehavePlus: Modeling system for predicting fire behavior
  • FARSITE: Fire Area Simulator for landscape-scale modeling
  • FlamMap: Spatial analysis of fire behavior across landscapes
  • FSPro: Fire Spread Probability for long-term risk assessment

Part 2: The Engineer’s Perspective

2.1 Fire-Resilient Design Principles

Building Materials and Construction

Fire-Resistant Materials

  • Class A roofing materials (asphalt shingles, metal, clay/concrete tiles)
  • Fire-rated siding (fiber cement, stucco, brick)
  • Tempered or multi-paned windows to resist breakage from heat
  • Non-combustible deck materials and attachments
  • Heat-resistant door assemblies

Structural Design Elements

  • Enclosed eaves to prevent ember entry
  • Screened vents with small mesh size (1/8 inch or smaller)
  • Gutter designs that minimize debris accumulation
  • Simplified roof forms with fewer valleys and intersections
  • Fire-rated walls and assemblies

Testing and Standards

  • ASTM E119: Fire tests for building construction materials
  • ASTM E2886: Testing for ember resistance of vents
  • NFPA 1144: Standard for Reducing Structure Ignition Hazards from Wildland Fire
  • ICC Wildland-Urban Interface Code requirements

Defensible Space Concept

Zone Concept

  • Zone 1: 0-5 feet (immediate zone) - most critical for structure survival
    • Non-combustible materials only (rock, pavers, concrete)
    • No vegetation against structure
  • Zone 2: 5-30 feet (intermediate zone)
    • Widely spaced vegetation
    • Low-growing, well-irrigated plants
    • No ladder fuels
  • Zone 3: 30-100+ feet (extended zone)
    • Thinned vegetation
    • Interrupted fuel continuity
    • Maintenance of tree spacing

Defensible Space Calculations

  • Area calculations for planning:
    • Rectangular area: $Area_{rectangle} = Length \times Width$
    • Circular area: $Area_{circle} = \pi r^2$
  • Vegetation spacing guidelines:
    • Vertical separation: 3x height of lower vegetation
    • Horizontal separation: 2-6x height of vegetation depending on slope

Water Supply and Access Systems

  • Dedicated fire-fighting water sources
  • Minimum flow rates and pressure requirements
  • Standpipe systems for multi-story buildings
  • Turnouts and turnarounds for emergency vehicles
  • Road width and gradient standards

2.2 Community-Scale Fire Engineering

Community Layout and Planning

Fire-Adapted Communities Concept

  • Integration of individual property protection, community protection, and ecosystem management
  • Focus on survivability rather than total fire exclusion
  • Emphasis on both physical infrastructure and social preparedness

Buffer Zones and Fuel Breaks

  • Strategic placement of low-flammability areas
  • Width requirements based on expected fire intensity
  • Types of fuel breaks:
    • Shaded fuel breaks (thinned forest)
    • Non-shaded breaks (complete vegetation removal)
    • Green belts (irrigated, low-flammability landscaping)

Infrastructure Hardening

  • Underground utilities to reduce ignition sources
  • Redundant power and water systems
  • Protected critical facilities (hospitals, emergency operations centers)
  • Evacuation route planning and maintenance

Fire Modeling and Simulation

Community Risk Assessment Tools

  • Fire spread modeling for community-scale analysis
  • Vulnerability assessment methodologies
  • Evacuation timing models
  • Cost-benefit analysis of mitigation measures

Performance-Based Design

  • Design based on predicted fire behavior rather than prescriptive codes
  • Fire scenario development and modeling
  • Probabilistic risk assessment
  • Integration with climate change projections

Engineering Calculations

  • Fire intensity predictions
  • Structure ignition probability
  • Evacuation timing calculations
  • Water supply requirements

Exterior Sprinkler Systems

Fire Detection and Monitoring

  • Early warning systems
  • Heat and smoke detection
  • Integration with community alert systems
  • Remote monitoring capabilities

Foam and Gel Fire Suppressants

  • Application methods and coverage rates
  • Duration of effectiveness
  • Environmental considerations
  • Cost-benefit analysis compared to water-only systems

Passive Fire Protection

Firebreaks and Fuel Modification

  • Natural and constructed firebreaks
  • Vegetation management strategies
  • Maintenance requirements and schedules
  • Integration with ecological objectives

Ember Resistance Measures

  • Screening and mesh systems
  • Gutter protection
  • Vent protection
  • Deck and porch enclosure

Thermal Barriers

  • Insulation types and fire ratings
  • Assembly design for fire resistance
  • Materials testing and certification

Part 3: The Policymaker’s Perspective

Regulatory Approaches

Building Codes

  • International Wildland-Urban Interface Code
  • California Building Code Chapter 7A
  • Local amendments to address specific risks
  • Enforcement mechanisms and compliance monitoring

Land Use Planning

  • Zoning for fire hazard areas
  • Subdivision regulations for fire-prone areas
  • Density limitations in high-risk zones
  • Required fire protection features for development approval

Retrofit Requirements

  • Mandatory vs. voluntary programs
  • Incentive structures
  • Trigger mechanisms (sale, renovation, disaster declaration)
  • Phasing strategies for community-wide implementation

Institutional Structures

Multi-Agency Coordination

  • Federal, state, and local roles and responsibilities
  • Incident command systems
  • Mutual aid agreements
  • Resource sharing protocols

Fire Governance Models

  • Fire protection districts
  • Community wildfire protection plans (CWPPs)
  • Fire safe councils
  • Homeowners’ associations with fire safety requirements

Emergency Management Integration

  • Integration of fire planning with all-hazards planning
  • Response, recovery, mitigation, and preparedness
  • Evacuation planning and management
  • Post-disaster recovery frameworks

Direct Costs

  • Evacuation and temporary housing costs

Indirect Costs

  • Economic disruption
  • Health impacts from smoke

Social Equity and Justice

Vulnerable Populations

Resource Allocation

Adaptive Governance

  • Integration of new science and technology

Conclusion

This unit demonstrates the complex interplay between natural processes, engineering solutions, and policy choices in addressing wildfire risk. As climate change increases wildfire frequency and intensity, communities need integrated approaches that combine scientific understanding of fire behavior, engineering strategies for structural protection, and policies that guide development patterns and emergency response. By viewing wildfire through these three interconnected lenses—scientist, engineer, and policymaker—we can develop more resilient communities that adapt to living with fire rather than simply trying to exclude it.
