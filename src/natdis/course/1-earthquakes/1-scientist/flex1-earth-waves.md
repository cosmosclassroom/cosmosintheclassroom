#### 1.1 The Big Picture: Plate Tectonics

**Earth's Dynamic Crust**  
The ground beneath our feet feels solid and permanent, but it's not. The entire surface of the Earth is like a giant, cracked eggshell. This shell, called the crust, is broken into huge, continent-sized pieces. 

![Earth's tectonic plates](./assets/images/tectonic-plates.png)

The foundational theory that describes how these pieces move and interact is called **Plate Tectonics**. Think of the continents as massive rafts floating on a slow-moving, hot, semi-solid layer of rock called the mantle. The motion of these plates, though incredibly slow (only a few centimeters per year, about the speed your fingernails grow), is the primary driver of earthquakes and volcanic activity.

**The Three Types of Plate Boundaries**  
If the plates are always moving, that means they must be interacting with each other along their edges, or boundaries.

These interactions happen in three main ways. At divergent boundaries, two plates are pulling apart from each other, allowing magma to rise and create new crust, like at the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. At convergent boundaries, two plates are colliding, which can cause one to slide beneath the other and push up massive mountain ranges like the Himalayas. Finally, at transform boundaries, two plates are sliding past each other horizontally, creating famous fault systems like California's San Andreas Fault. Each of these boundary types creates different kinds of stresses on the Earth's crust.

![Types of plate boundaries: divergent, convergent, transform](./assets/images/plate-boundaries.png)

**Fault Lines: Where the Action Happens**  
All this pushing, pulling, and sliding creates cracks in the crust. A fault is any fracture in the Earth's rock where movement has occurred. While plate boundaries are the largest faults, smaller faults can exist all over the crust. It is along these fault lines that the vast majority of the world's earthquakes occur.

![Fault lines and earthquake zones](./assets/images/fault-lines.png)

**From Stress to Strain: The Elastic Rebound Theory**  
The movement of tectonic plates is not smooth. The rough, massive edges of the plates get stuck against each other, and as the plates continue to try to move, an incredible amount of stress builds up in the rock. The rock actually bends and deforms, storing energy like a stretched rubber band.

Eventually, the stress becomes too great, and the rock suddenly breaks and snaps back to its original shape. This sudden release of stored energy is an earthquake. The waves of energy that travel outward from this break are what we feel as shaking. This natural process—the storing and sudden releasing of energy along a fault—is the fundamental Hazard that scientists work to understand.

![Elastic rebound theory diagram](./assets/images/elastic-rebound.png)

#### 1.2 Reading the Waves: The Science of Seismology

**Body Waves vs. Surface Waves**  
The energy released during an earthquake travels away from the fault in the form of waves. We call these **Seismic Waves**. Scientists are most interested in the first two types that travel through the body of the Earth, because they give us the clues we need to find an earthquake's origin. The first to arrive are **P-waves** (Primary waves), which are the fastest. They travel by compressing and expanding the rock in the same direction the wave is moving, like a Slinky being pushed and pulled. Arriving after them are the slower **S-waves** (Secondary waves), which move the rock up and down or side to side, perpendicular to the direction the wave is traveling, like flicking a rope.

![Seismic waves: P-waves and S-waves](./assets/images/seismic-waves.png)

**The S-P Interval: A Race Against Time**  
Imagine two runners starting a race at the same time, but one runs faster than the other. The farther they get from the starting line, the bigger the gap between them will be. Seismic waves work the same way. P-waves are the fast runners, and S-waves are the slower runners. Both start at the earthquake's origin at the same time.

When seismographs—the instruments that detect earthquakes—record the shaking, they first see the P-wave arrive, and then some time later, the S-wave arrives. The time gap between them is called the **S-P interval**. By measuring this time gap, scientists can calculate the distance to the earthquake's starting point. A short gap means the earthquake is close; a long gap means it's far away.

![Seismograph showing P-wave and S-wave arrivals](./assets/images/seismograph-sp-interval.png)

**Triangulation: Finding "X" on the Map**  
Knowing the distance is helpful, but it doesn't tell you the direction. If a seismograph in your town detects an earthquake 100 miles away, it could be 100 miles north, south, east, or west. To find the exact location, you need data from at least three different seismograph stations.

Imagine drawing a circle around each of the three stations, where the radius of each circle is the calculated distance to the earthquake. The single point where all three circles intersect is the earthquake's **Epicenter**—the point on the Earth's surface directly above where the earthquake began. This process is called **triangulation**.

![Triangulation to locate an epicenter](./assets/images/triangulation.png)

**Measuring an Earthquake: Magnitude vs. Impact**  
Scientists need a way to measure the strength of an earthquake at its source. They call this **magnitude**. Think of it like the wattage of a light bulb—it's a measure of the total energy released. A magnitude 7.0 earthquake releases about 32 times more energy than a magnitude 6.0.

But the magnitude doesn't tell the whole story. The actual damage an earthquake causes—its **impact**—depends on a community's **Vulnerability**. This includes factors like how close people are to the epicenter, what their buildings are made of, and how prepared they are. A powerful, high-magnitude earthquake in a remote desert might have very little impact, while a weaker, lower-magnitude earthquake in the center of a dense, unprepared city could be a catastrophe. Understanding the difference between the hazard's strength and a community's vulnerability is the key to understanding risk.

![Magnitude vs. impact: city vs. desert](./assets/images/magnitude-vs-impact.png)
