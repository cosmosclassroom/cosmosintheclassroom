# Unit 2: Tsunamis - Lecture Notes

## Overview
This unit explores how coastal communities can design defenses that protect both lives and ecosystems from the ocean's power. Using the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami as a global case study, we examine the cascading impacts of major tsunami events and evaluate the effectiveness of different coastal defense strategies, from engineered sea walls to natural barriers.

## Part 1: The Scientist's Perspective

### 1.1 Tsunami Generation and Physics

#### Key Concepts
- **Tsunami**: A series of ocean waves typically caused by sudden displacement of the seafloor during earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, or landslides
- **Bathymetry**: The measurement and mapping of the ocean floor depths
- **Wave Shoaling**: The process by which waves slow down and increase in height as they move into shallower water
- **Wave Period**: The time interval between successive wave crests (tsunamis have much longer periods than wind-driven waves)
- **Wave Amplitude**: The vertical distance from the still water level to the wave crest

#### Mechanisms of Tsunami Generation

**Seismic Tsunamis**
1. Vertical displacement of the seafloor during an earthquake
2. Water column displacement creates potential energy
3. Gravity causes water to move horizontally away from the displacement
4. Wave train propagates across the ocean

**Other Generation Mechanisms**
- **Submarine Landslides**: Displacement of large volumes of sediment
- **Volcanic Activity**: Eruptions, caldera collapses, or flank failures
- **Meteorological Events**: Rare atmospheric disturbances (meteotsunami)
- **Asteroid Impact**: Extremely rare but potentially catastrophic

#### Wave Propagation and Behavior

**Deep Ocean Propagation**
- Tsunami waves travel as shallow water waves (wavelength >> water depth)
- Wave height in deep ocean is relatively small (often < 1m)
- Wave speed depends on water depth: $Wave\,Speed = \sqrt{g \cdot Depth}$
- Waves can travel across oceans at speeds of 500-1000 km/h
- Minimal energy loss during deep-water propagation

**Coastal Transformation**
- As waves approach shore, they slow down due to decreasing depth
- Energy conservation causes wave height to increase dramatically
- **Wave Shoaling**: $Height_{shallow} = Height_{deep} \cdot \left(\frac{Depth_{deep}}{Depth_{shallow}}\right)^{1/4}$
- **Energy Relationship**: $Energy \propto (Wave\,Height)^2$
- Coastal configuration (bays, inlets) can focus or disperse wave energy

### 1.2 Tsunami Detection and Warning Systems

#### Detection Technologies
- **Deep-ocean Assessment and Reporting of Tsunamis (DART) buoys**
  - Bottom pressure recorders detect passing tsunami waves
  - Data relayed to warning centers in real-time
- **Tide gauges** monitor sea level changes at coastlines
- **Seismic networks** provide initial earthquake detection and warnings
- **Satellite altimetry** can sometimes detect tsunami waves in open ocean

#### Warning Systems
- **Regional warning centers** evaluate tsunami risk following earthquakes
- Warning time depends on distance from tsunami source
  - Local tsunamis: minutes
  - Distant tsunamis: hours
- Forecasting models predict wave arrival times and heights
- Communication systems to disseminate warnings

#### Tsunami Modeling
- **Propagation models**: Track tsunami waves across oceans
- **Inundation models**: Predict flooding of coastal areas
- **Numerical simulation**: Combines bathymetry, topography, and generation mechanics
- **Arrival time calculation**: $Tsunami\,Arrival\,Time = \frac{Distance}{Wave\,Speed}$

### 1.3 The 2011 Tōhoku Tsunami Case Study

#### Event Timeline
- Magnitude 9.0-9.1 earthquake off Japan's east coast (March 11, 2011)
- Epicenter 70 km east of Tōhoku region
- Vertical seafloor displacement up to 10 meters
- Tsunami waves reached Japanese coast within 30 minutes
- Maximum wave heights of 40+ meters in some locations

#### Scientific Observations
- Unexpected fault displacement pattern
- Tsunami exceeded hazard predictions
- Complex wave behavior due to coastal topography
- Transoceanic propagation affected distant coastlines
- Multiple wave arrivals over several hours

#### Impacts and Lessons
- Nearly 16,000 deaths (majority from tsunami, not earthquake)
- Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster
- Economic damage exceeding $360 billion
- Revealed limitations in hazard assessment
- Led to revisions of tsunami hazard models globally

## Part 2: The Engineer's Perspective

### 2.1 Coastal Engineering Approaches

#### Fundamental Coastal Processes
- **Wave-structure interaction**: How waves behave when encountering barriers
- **Sediment transport**: Movement of sand and other materials by waves
- **Erosion and accretion**: Removal and deposition of coastal materials
- **Storm surge**: Temporary sea level rise during storms (different from tsunamis)
- **Coastal morphodynamics**: How coastlines change shape over time

#### Assessment of Coastal Vulnerability
- **Exposure**: Population and infrastructure in tsunami-prone areas
- **Physical vulnerability**: Structural resistance to wave forces
- **Functional vulnerability**: Critical systems and services
- **Topographic vulnerability**: Low-lying areas, confined bays
- **Social vulnerability**: Evacuation capacity, awareness, demographic factors

#### Engineering Design Considerations
- **Wave forces**: Both hydrostatic and hydrodynamic
- **Debris impact**: Floating objects carried by tsunami waves
- **Scour**: Erosion around foundations
- **Hydraulic considerations**: Flow velocity, flow depth, and duration
- **Multi-hazard approach**: Combined effects of earthquake and tsunami

### 2.2 "Hard" Engineering Solutions

#### Seawalls and Barriers
- Vertical or sloped concrete structures designed to block waves
- Height determined by design tsunami height
- Foundations must resist scour and liquefaction
- Often incorporate wave-dissipating features
- Examples: Japan's concrete seawalls, Kamaishi tsunami breakwater

#### Tsunami Gates and Floodgates
- Movable barriers that can be closed when tsunami warnings are issued
- Allow normal waterway use during non-emergency times
- Require reliable operating systems and warning time
- Examples: Tokyo Bay gates, Venice MOSE project

#### Reinforced Coastal Buildings
- Tsunami-resistant building codes
- Structural systems that can withstand hydrodynamic forces
- Elevated first floors above expected inundation level
- Breakaway walls on lower levels
- Foundation designs resistant to scour

#### Limitations of Hard Engineering
- High construction and maintenance costs
- Environmental impacts (altered coastal processes)
- Visual/aesthetic impacts
- False sense of security ("safety paradox")
- Cannot completely eliminate risk from extreme events

### 2.3 "Living" or Nature-Based Solutions

#### Coastal Vegetation and Forests
- Mangrove forests, coastal woodlands
- Reduce wave energy through friction and breaking
- Trap debris that might otherwise damage structures
- Stabilize coastal sediments
- Provide ecological benefits beyond tsunami protection

#### Artificial Reefs and Offshore Features
- Submerged structures that cause waves to break offshore
- Reduce wave energy reaching the coast
- Can be designed to enhance marine habitat
- Often combined with other protective measures

#### Dunes and Beach Nourishment
- Natural and engineered sand dunes as barriers
- Sacrificial systems that absorb wave energy
- Require periodic maintenance and restoration
- Provide recreational and ecological benefits

#### Hybrid Approaches
- Combination of hard and nature-based solutions
- "Building with nature" design philosophy
- Ecological engineering to enhance resilience
- Examples: sills with marsh creation, terraced shorelines

#### Evaluation Metrics
- **Wave energy reduction**: Percentage of energy dissipated
- **Ecological function**: Habitat value, biodiversity support
- **Cost-effectiveness**: Initial and lifecycle costs versus benefits
- **Adaptability**: Ability to adjust to changing conditions
- **Co-benefits**: Additional ecosystem services provided

## Part 3: The Policymaker's Perspective

### 3.1 Land Use Planning for Tsunami Resilience

#### Hazard Zoning
- Tsunami hazard maps based on inundation modeling
- Risk-informed zoning regulations
- Designated evacuation areas and routes
- Critical infrastructure siting outside high-risk zones
- Buffer zones between ocean and development

#### Building Codes and Standards
- Tsunami-specific design requirements
- Performance-based engineering standards
- Vertical evacuation structures in high-risk areas
- Retrofitting requirements for existing structures
- Implementation and enforcement mechanisms

#### Managed Retreat Strategies
- Planned relocation from high-risk areas
- Buyout programs for vulnerable properties
- Land acquisition and conservation
- Gradual transition to less intensive coastal uses
- Balance between property rights and public safety

### 3.2 Socioeconomic and Ethical Dimensions

#### Cost-Benefit Considerations
- Direct costs: Protection infrastructure, building adaptations
- Indirect costs: Economic disruption, environmental impacts
- Benefits: Lives saved, property protected, business continuity
- Distributional effects: Who pays and who benefits
- Time horizons: Present versus future costs and benefits

#### Community Engagement and Perception
- Risk communication strategies
- Local knowledge integration
- Cultural attachment to place
- Public participation in planning
- Building community acceptance of protective measures

#### Ethical Frameworks
- Equity considerations in resource allocation
- Intergenerational justice and long-term planning
- Rights-based approaches to safety
- Cost-sharing between beneficiaries and broader society
- Balancing individual freedoms and collective security

### 3.3 Integrated Coastal Management

#### Multi-stakeholder Governance
- Coordination among government levels (local, regional, national)
- Public-private partnerships
- Community-based disaster risk management
- International cooperation for transboundary tsunamis
- Adaptive governance systems

#### Early Warning and Evacuation
- Public education and awareness campaigns
- Warning dissemination technologies
- Evacuation planning and drills
- Special provisions for vulnerable populations
- Integration with broader emergency management systems

#### Insurance and Financial Mechanisms
- Risk transfer through insurance markets
- Catastrophe bonds and other financial instruments
- Incentives for risk reduction
- Public insurance programs for uninsurable risks
- Post-disaster recovery funding

#### Adaptive Management
- Monitoring and evaluation of protection measures
- Periodic reassessment of tsunami hazards
- Flexible planning frameworks
- Learning from disasters and near-misses
- Adjusting strategies based on new information

## Conclusion

This unit demonstrates the importance of integrating scientific understanding, engineering solutions, and policy approaches to create tsunami-resilient coastal communities. By examining the Tōhoku tsunami and other case studies, we understand that effective tsunami protection requires a comprehensive approach that considers not only physical barriers but also natural systems, land use planning, warning systems, and community preparedness. The choices coastal communities make involve trade-offs between immediate costs, long-term security, environmental impacts, and quality of life considerations.
