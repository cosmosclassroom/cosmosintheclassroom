9 Electromagnetism and Light

Characteristics of Light

Light - the range of electromagnetic radiation that is visible to the human eye

Electromagnetic Spectrum - the range of all wavelengths/frequencies of electromagnetic radiation

Electromagnetic Radiation - waves produced by synchronization of oscillations between electric and magnetic fields

Caused by the movement (oscillations) of charged particles

EM waves vary depending on frequency and wavelength

EM waves are composed of a wave of oscillating, perpendicular electric and magnetic fields

All EM waves move at the speed of light

In a vacuum, c = 3.00 x 108 m/s

Wave directions of travel can be approximated as rays

Flat Mirrors

Light traveling through a uniform substance, air, water, or a vacuum, always travels in a straight line

Reflection of light is the change in direction of a light ray deflected by a surface

The texture of a surface affects how it reflects light

In flat surfaces, incoming and reflected angles are equal (relative to a normal line)

A good mirror can reflect about 90% of incoming light, but no surface is a perfect reflector

Angle of Incidence - angle at which incoming light strikes a surface

Angle of Reflection - the angle at which reflected light leaves a surface

Both angles are measured relative to a line perpendicular to the reflected surface at the point of contact

Ray diagrams help predict the locations of images formed

In a flat mirror, reflected rays appear to come from a location on the other side of the mirror

Object distance (p) is the distance between the object and the mirror

Image distance (q) is the (apparent) distance of the object “behind” the mirror

On a flat mirror, the object height (h) is the same as the image height (h’)

Virtual image - an image formed by rays that appear to come from the image point behind the mirror (but never really do)

Flat mirrors always produce virtual images

Curved Mirrors

Concave mirrors can be used to form real images, and can  be projected onto films or screens

Concave mirrors can change the size and orientation of an object as needed

This is adjusted by changing the radius of curvature, C

The radius of curvature, C, is the same as the radius of the spherical shell of which the mirror is a small part

R is therefore the distance from the mirror’s surface to the center of curvature, C

Principal Axis - an infinite line extending from the center of the mirror’s surface and through the center of curvature, C

Image location can be predicted with the mirror equation

For a spherical mirror, the focal length is equal to half the radius of curvature

Concave mirrors are used whenever a magnified image is needed

Magnification relates image and object sizes

Images created by spherical mirrors suffer from spherical aberration

Wave-Particle Duality

Particles

The Photoelectric Effect - when light shines on a surface, it can knock electrons loose and create an electric current

The energy of the ejected electrons matches the frequency but not the amplitude of the light

These results are consistent with light behaving as a particle called a photon

Compton Scattering - shooting X-rays at a thin layer of metal produces a scattering consistent with the conservation of momentum, treating the light as photon particles
