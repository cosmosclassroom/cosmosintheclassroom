### Electromagnetism

Light \- the range of electromagnetic radiation that is visible to the human eye

Caused by the movement (oscillations) of charged particles

### Batteries

**Luigi Galvani**  
Around 1791, Luigi Galvani found that frog legs could twitch if static electricity was applied to them  
He found they could also be made to twitch by injection with mixtures of metals  
Galvani assumed that the metals acted like conductors for the frog’s “life force”   
**Alessandro Volta** was skeptical of Galvani’s explanation, predicting instead that the metals were the source of the electricity and that the liquid in the frog tissues helped conduct it  
He was able to demonstrate that frog legs are actually very sensitive instruments for detecting what he called *electromotive force*, which we now call voltage  
To test his hypothesis, Volta placed discs of zinc and silver in columns separated by cloth or paper soaked in salt or acid  
These “piles” or “batteries” produced a much larger potential difference than induction or static electricity  
The voltage was so strong that connecting conductors to either side of the columns could force a spark to leap through the air  
An **electric battery** is a source of nearly constant **potential difference** by transforming chemical energy into electric energy  
A simple battery consists of two **electrodes** made of different materials immersed in a solution or paste known as an **electrolyte**  
An assembly of two electrodes dissolved in the electrolyte is called a **cell**, and batteries are composed of one or more cells  
The electrolyte dissolves one of the electrodes, releasing electrons into the conductor and positive ions into solution  
The charge of this solution attracts the electrons from the other electrode, causing the second electrode to carry a positive charge  
If charge is allowed to flow through the terminals by a conductor, the chemical reaction continues and a flow of electrons through the conductor is sustainable  
The voltage produced depends on the materials of the electrons and their abilities to be dissolved by the acid  
Multiple cells can be connected between the positive terminal of one and the negative terminal of another  
These cells are said to be *in series***,** and their combined voltage is the algebraic sum of the voltage of each.

## Voltage 

Also known as electric potential difference, **voltage** is a measure of the electric potential energy per unit charge between two points in a circuit  
Voltage is the “push” that drives electric charge through a conductor (rather than being distributed evenly as in statics)  
Voltage provides the energy needed for charges to move or flow, similarly to water pressure  
Voltage is measured in **volts** (V)

# Lecture Notes: Electric Potential Energy, Voltage, Current, Resistance, and Power

1. ## History

   Luigi Galvani (1737–1798) was an Italian physician and physicist who conducted pioneering experiments on bioelectricity. He discovered that the muscles of dissected frogs twitched when touched with a metal instrument, leading him to propose the existence of "animal electricity."  
   Alessandro Volta (1745–1827), also an Italian physicist, challenged Galvani's theory and demonstrated that the source of the electric current in Galvani's experiments was not the animal tissue but the metal electrodes. Volta invented the voltaic pile, the first chemical battery, which produced a steady source of electric current.  
   Georg Simon Ohm (1789–1854), a German physicist, formulated Ohm's Law, which describes the relationship between voltage, current, and resistance in electrical circuits. His work laid the foundation for understanding the behavior of electric currents in conductors.  
   

1. ## Energy Stored in a Battery:

The energy stored in a battery can be calculated using the formula \\( E \= Q \\cdot V \\), where \\( Q \\) is the total charge stored in the battery and \\( V \\) is the potential difference provided by the battery.  
   The energy stored in a battery is measured in joules (J).

3. ## Understanding Voltage:

   Voltage, also known as electric potential difference, is the measure of the potential energy difference per unit charge between two points in an electric field.

   It represents the work needed to move a charge from one point to another against an electric field.

   Voltage is measured in volts (V), where 1 volt is equivalent to 1 joule per coulomb (1 V \= 1 J/C).

## 

## 4\. Electric Current and Charge:

   Electric current is the flow of electric charge.  
   The total charge that passes through a circuit can be calculated using the formula \\( Q \= I \\cdot t \\), where \\( I \\) is the current and \\( t \\) is the time.  
   Charge is measured in coulombs (C).

## 5\. Voltage Drop Across a Resistor:

   When a current flows through a resistor, a potential difference or voltage drop is experienced.  
   The voltage drop across a resistor can be calculated using Ohm's Law: \\( V \= I \\cdot R \\), where \\( V \\) is the voltage drop, \\( I \\) is the current, and \\( R \\) is the resistance.  
   Voltage is measured in volts (V).

## 6\. Resistance and Ohm's Law:

   Resistance is a measure of how much a material opposes the flow of electric current.  
   Resistors are passive electrical components designed to introduce resistance into an electric circuit.  
   Ohm's Law states that the current through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage across the two points and inversely proportional to the resistance.  
   The resistance of a resistor can be calculated using \\( R \= V / I \\), where \\( R \\) is the resistance, \\( V \\) is the voltage, and \\( I \\) is the current.  
   Resistance is measured in ohms (\\( \\Omega \\)).

## 7\. Function of a Resistor:

   A resistor functions to regulate or limit the flow of electric current in a circuit.  
   By introducing resistance into a circuit, resistors control the amount of current that can pass through them.  
   Resistors are used to reduce current flow, divide voltage, or provide a specific voltage drop in a circuit.  
   They are commonly used for purposes such as controlling the brightness of LEDs, setting the gain in amplifier circuits, and providing biasing in transistor circuits.

## 8\. Electrical Power and Operation of Appliances:

   Electrical power is the rate at which electrical energy is transferred or converted into other forms of energy.  
   Power in an electrical circuit can be calculated using the formula \\( P \= V \\cdot I \\), where \\( P \\) is power, \\( V \\) is voltage, and \\( I \\) is current.  
   Power is measured in watts (W).  
   Electrical appliances operate by converting electrical power into other forms of energy.   
Examples:  
     Light bulbs: Convert electrical power into light and heat energy.  
     Heaters: Convert electrical power into heat energy.  
     Motors: Convert electrical power into mechanical energy to perform tasks such as rotating a fan or driving a conveyor belt.  
   The power consumed by an electrical appliance depends on its voltage and current requirements. Appliances with higher power ratings require more electrical energy to operate and may have higher voltage and current demands.  
   Understanding power consumption is essential for selecting the appropriate electrical components and designing efficient electrical systems.

## 9\. Calculating Power Consumed:

   To determine the power consumed by a device, multiply the voltage across the device by the current flowing through it.  
   \\( P \= V \\cdot I \\), where \\( P \\) is power, \\( V \\) is voltage, and \\( I \\) is current.

Understanding the contributions of Galvani, Volta, and Ohm provides context for the development of the principles of electricity and serves as the foundation for understanding electric potential energy, voltage, current, resistance, and power in high school physics.
