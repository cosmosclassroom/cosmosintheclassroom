# Chapter 10: Fundamentals of Light and Reflection

## 10.1 Nature and Properties of Light

Have you ever watched sunlight streaming through your window, creating patterns on the floor? Or marveled at how a rainbow appears after a storm? The light that enables us to see the world around us is one of the most fascinating phenomena in physics. But what exactly is light?

### Wave-Particle Duality

For centuries, scientists debated whether light was a wave or a particle. Isaac Newton championed the particle theory, while Christian Huygens argued it was a wave. The surprising truth? Light is both.

This concept of wave-particle duality might seem contradictory, but it's one of the most profound discoveries in modern physics. Light sometimes behaves as a wave, spreading out and interfering with itself. Other times, it acts as a particle—a discrete packet of energy called a photon.

The mathematical relationship that connects these dual aspects is:

$$E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$

Where:
- $E$ is the energy of a photon (J)
- $h$ is Planck's constant ($6.63 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s)
- $f$ is the frequency (Hz)
- $c$ is the speed of light ($3 \times 10^8$ m/s)
- $\lambda$ is the wavelength (m)

This elegant equation, formulated by Einstein based on Planck's work, shows that higher frequency (shorter wavelength) light carries more energy per photon. This explains why ultraviolet light can damage skin while visible light is harmless at similar intensities—UV photons pack more energy!

### Electromagnetic Spectrum

The light we can see—visible light—is just a tiny sliver of a vast electromagnetic spectrum. From radio waves that can be kilometers long to gamma rays with wavelengths smaller than an atom, all electromagnetic radiation travels at the same speed in vacuum: 300,000,000 meters per second (the famous constant $c$).

The relationship between frequency and wavelength across this spectrum is described by:

$$c = \lambda f$$

This equation remains constant throughout the electromagnetic spectrum. If you know either the frequency or wavelength, you can calculate the other.

The visible spectrum ranges from approximately 400 nanometers (violet) to 700 nanometers (red). Beyond the violet end lies ultraviolet light, X-rays, and gamma rays. Past the red end are infrared radiation, microwaves, and radio waves. Each type of radiation has unique properties and applications, from the radio waves that carry your favorite music to the X-rays that help doctors see inside your body.

### Speed, Frequency, and Wavelength

Light travels through vacuum at a blistering speed of approximately 300,000,000 meters per second—fast enough to circle Earth more than seven times in a single second! This speed is represented by the constant $c$ in equations.

However, light slows down when traveling through matter. The degree to which a material slows down light is quantified by its refractive index ($n$):

$$v = \frac{c}{n}$$

When light enters a medium with refractive index $n$, its wavelength changes while its frequency remains constant:

$$\lambda_{\text{medium}} = \frac{\lambda_{\text{vacuum}}}{n}$$

These relationships explain why light bends when passing between different materials—a phenomenon called refraction that we'll explore more in the next chapter.

## 10.2 Reflection Principles

When you look in a mirror each morning or see your reflection in a still pond, you're observing one of light's fundamental behaviors: reflection. Let's explore the mathematical principles that govern this everyday phenomenon.

### Law of Reflection

The law of reflection is beautifully simple: the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection. Both angles are measured from the normal—an imaginary line perpendicular to the reflecting surface at the point where the light ray hits.

Mathematically, we express this as:

$$\theta_i = \theta_r$$

Where:
- $\theta_i$ is the angle of incidence (degrees or radians)
- $\theta_r$ is the angle of reflection (degrees or radians)

This relationship holds true whether light reflects from a precision-crafted mirror or a rough surface like paper. The difference is that on rough surfaces, the microscopic bumps cause light rays to reflect in many different directions, creating diffuse reflection rather than the clear image we see in mirrors (specular reflection).

### Total Internal Reflection

Have you ever noticed how a diamond seems to trap and redirect light, creating its characteristic sparkle? Or wondered how fiber optic cables transmit information using light? Both rely on a fascinating phenomenon called total internal reflection.

When light travels from a denser medium (like glass) to a less dense medium (like air), something remarkable happens if the angle is steep enough: instead of passing through, the light reflects back completely! This occurs when the angle of incidence exceeds what's called the critical angle.

The critical angle can be calculated using:

$$\sin \theta_c = \frac{n_2}{n_1}$$

Where:
- $\theta_c$ is the critical angle
- $n_1$ is the refractive index of the first medium (higher value)
- $n_2$ is the refractive index of the second medium (lower value)

For angles greater than the critical angle, we get total internal reflection, where all light energy is reflected with no loss—a property that makes fiber optics possible. The condition for total internal reflection is:

$$\sin \theta_i > \frac{n_2}{n_1}$$

This principle allows fiber optic cables to transmit data-carrying light signals over vast distances with minimal loss, forming the backbone of our modern internet infrastructure.



## 10.3 Practice Problems: Reflection Calculations

Now that we've explored the fundamental properties of light and the principles of reflection, let's apply these concepts through some practical problems. Working through these calculations will deepen your understanding and prepare you for more advanced optical concepts.

### Problem Set 10.1: Wave Properties of Light

1. Calculate the frequency of yellow light with a wavelength of 580 nm.

2. If blue light has a frequency of $6.5 \times 10^{14}$ Hz, what is its wavelength in vacuum?

3. Red light with a wavelength of 650 nm in air enters water with a refractive index of 1.33. Calculate its wavelength in water.

4. A photon has an energy of $3.5 \times 10^{-19}$ J. What is its frequency and wavelength?

5. Compare the energy of a photon of violet light (wavelength 400 nm) to a photon of red light (wavelength 700 nm).

### Problem Set 10.2: Laws of Reflection

1. A light ray strikes a plane mirror at an angle of 35° to the normal. What is the angle of reflection?

2. Two plane mirrors are placed at an angle of 60° to each other. If a light ray strikes the first mirror at an angle of 25° to the normal, at what angle will it emerge after reflecting from the second mirror?

3. A periscope uses two mirrors at 45° angles. Trace the path of a horizontal light ray through this system.

4. A light ray reflects off three mirrors arranged in a triangular configuration with interior angles of 60°, 60°, and 60°. If the ray initially strikes the first mirror at an angle of 30° to the normal, what will be its final direction after all three reflections?

5. A laser beam strikes a rotating mirror that completes one rotation every 2 seconds. If the beam reflects onto a wall 5 meters away, calculate the speed of the reflected spot on the wall.

### Problem Set 10.3: Total Internal Reflection

1. Calculate the critical angle for total internal reflection at a glass-air interface where the refractive index of glass is 1.52.

2. Light travels from water (n = 1.33) to air (n = 1.00). At what angle of incidence will the light be totally reflected?

3. An optical fiber has a core with refractive index 1.48 and a cladding with refractive index 1.46. Calculate the critical angle for total internal reflection at the core-cladding interface.

4. A diver underwater looks up at the water surface. At what angle from the vertical will the diver see total internal reflection begin to occur?

5. Design an equilateral prism made of crown glass (n = 1.52) that uses total internal reflection to redirect light by 90°. At what range of incident angles will the prism function as intended?

### Answers to Selected Problems:

10.1.1. $f = \frac{c}{\lambda} = \frac{3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}}{580 \times 10^{-9} \text{ m}} = 5.17 \times 10^{14} \text{ Hz}$

10.2.1. The angle of reflection is 35°, equal to the angle of incidence.

10.3.1. $\sin \theta_c = \frac{n_{\text{air}}}{n_{\text{glass}}} = \frac{1.00}{1.52} = 0.658 \implies \theta_c = 41.1°$
