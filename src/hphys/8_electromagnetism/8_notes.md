# Electric Charge and Fields - Lecture Notes

## I. The Nature of Electric Charge

### A. Fundamental Properties
- Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter (like mass)
- Two types: positive (+) and negative (-)
- Key difference from mass: can attract OR repel

### B. Quantization of Charge
- Charge comes in discrete packets, not continuous amounts
- Elementary charge: $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C (smallest natural unit)
- All charges are integer multiples of $e$
- Carriers: electrons ($-e$), protons ($+e$)

### C. Charge Conservation
- Total charge in isolated system stays constant
- Charge can transfer between objects but never created/destroyed
- Applies to all processes: chemical reactions, nuclear decay, etc.

### D. Methods of Charge Transfer
- **Friction**: rubbing materials strips electrons (static electricity)
- **Chemical reactions**: batteries separate charges electrochemically  
- **Conduction**: free movement of charges in conductors
- **Induction**: temporary charge separation using external fields

## II. Coulomb's Law - Force Between Charges

### A. Basic Relationship
Charles-Augustin de Coulomb (1785) discovered the mathematical law governing electric forces

$$F = k\frac{|q_1q_2|}{r^2}$$

where $k = 8.99 \times 10^9$ N·m²/C² (Coulomb's constant)

### B. Key Features
- Force directly proportional to product of charges
- Force inversely proportional to square of distance (inverse square law)
- Like charges repel, opposite charges attract
- Same mathematical form as Newton's gravitational law

### C. Magnitude Comparison
- Electric forces $\sim 10^{36}$ times stronger than gravitational forces
- This explains why atoms hold together despite small masses

### D. Problem-Solving Approach
1. Identify charges ($q_1$, $q_2$) and distance ($r$)
2. Calculate $|q_1q_2|$ (product of magnitudes)
3. Square the distance ($r^2$)
4. Apply formula: $F = k\frac{|q_1q_2|}{r^2}$
5. Determine direction (attractive vs repulsive)

## III. Electric Field Concept

### A. What is an Electric Field?
- Region of space where electric charges experience forces
- Think of it as an "alteration" or "disturbance" of space around charges
- Allows us to describe electric influences without "action at a distance"

### B. Field from Point Charge
$$E = k\frac{|q|}{r^2}$$

- Field radiates outward from positive charges
- Field points inward toward negative charges  
- Strength decreases with distance (inverse square relationship)

### C. Field Line Visualization
- Imaginary lines showing direction a positive test charge would move
- Density of lines indicates field strength
- Never cross each other
- Start on positive charges, end on negative charges

---

## IV. Electric Field Patterns

### A. Common Configurations
- **Single point charge**: radial pattern (outward for +, inward for -)
- **Two like charges**: field lines repel, creating "prohibited" region between
- **Two unlike charges (dipole)**: field lines connect positive to negative
- **Uniform field**: parallel, equally-spaced lines (between charged plates)

### B. Superposition Principle
- Total field = vector sum of individual fields
- Fields add as vectors at each point in space
- Allows analysis of complex charge distributions

## V. Electric Potential and Voltage

### A. Definition of Electric Potential
$$V = \frac{PE_{electric}}{q}$$

- Electric potential energy per unit charge
- Units: Joules per Coulomb = Volts (V)
- Tells us how much energy per coulomb is available

### B. Physical Meaning
- Voltage represents energy difference between two points
- Higher potential = more energy per unit charge
- Charges naturally move from high to low potential

### C. Potential from Point Charge
$$V = \frac{kq}{r}$$

- Positive charges create positive potential
- Negative charges create negative potential
- Potential decreases with distance

### D. Equipotential Lines
- Lines where potential is constant
- Always perpendicular to electric field lines
- No work required to move charge along equipotential line
- Like contour lines on topographic maps

## VI. Energy Storage in Electric Fields

### A. Field Energy Concept
- Energy can be stored directly in electric fields
- When charges are separated against natural forces, work is done
- This work energy gets "stored" in the resulting electric field
- Energy can later be released when charges move back together

### B. Capacitors - Practical Energy Storage
- Device with two conducting plates separated by gap
- Battery moves charge from one plate to other, creating field between plates
- Energy stored in the electric field between plates

### C. Capacitor Energy Formula
$$PE_e = \frac{1}{2}CV^2$$

where:
- $C$ = capacitance (ability to store charge per volt)
- $V$ = voltage across plates

### D. Factors Affecting Capacitance
- **Plate area**: larger area → more capacitance
- **Plate separation**: closer plates → more capacitance  
- **Dielectric material**: insulating material between plates can increase capacitance

### E. Applications
- Camera flashes (rapid energy release)
- Defibrillators (controlled energy delivery)
- Electronic filters and timing circuits
- Power storage systems

## VII. Key Relationships Summary

### A. Fundamental Equations
- **Coulomb's Law**: $F = k\frac{|q_1q_2|}{r^2}$
- **Electric Field**: $E = k\frac{|q|}{r^2}$  
- **Electric Potential**: $V = \frac{kq}{r}$
- **Capacitor Energy**: $PE_e = \frac{1}{2}CV^2$

### B. Conceptual Flow
1. **Charges** create forces (Coulomb's Law)
2. **Forces** can be described using **fields** (simplifies complex situations)
3. **Fields** have associated **potential energy** (voltage concept)
4. **Energy** can be stored directly in the **fields themselves**

### C. Units to Remember
- Charge: Coulombs (C)
- Force: Newtons (N)  
- Electric Field: N/C or V/m
- Potential: Volts (V)
- Energy: Joules (J)
- Capacitance: Farads (F)
