# Wave-Particle Bridge: Answer Key

##### Problem 1: Photon Energy Calculations

**Problem Statement:**  
Calculate the energy of photons with different frequencies and compare them.

###### Part a) Energy of blue light photon (7.5 × 10¹⁴ Hz)

**Starting Formula:** 

$$E = hf$$ (Photon energy formula)

**Solution:**

$$E = h \times f = (6.63 \times 10^{-34} \,J \cdot s) \times (7.5 \times 10^{14} \text{ Hz})$$
$$E = 4.97 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}$$

**Converting to electron-volts:**

$$E \text{ in eV} = \frac{E \text{ in J}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J/eV}} = \frac{4.97 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J/eV}} = 3.11 \text{ eV}$$

###### Part b) Energy of red light photon (4.3 × 10¹⁴ Hz)

**Starting Formula:** $$E = hf$$ (Photon energy formula)

**Solution:**  

$$E = h \times f = (6.63 \times 10^{-34} \,J \cdot s) \times (4.3 \times 10^{14} \text{ Hz})$$

$$E = 2.85 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}$$

**Converting to electron-volts:**  

$$E \text{ in eV} = \frac{E \text{ in J}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J/eV}} = \frac{2.85 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J/eV}} = 1.78 \text{ eV}$$

**Comparison:**  
$$\frac{E_{\text{blue}}}{E_{\text{red}}} = \frac{3.11 \text{ eV}}{1.78 \text{ eV}} \approx 1.75$$

Blue photons have about 1.75 times the energy of red photons, confirming that higher frequency light carries more energy per photon.

##### Problem 2: Radio Wave Analysis

**Problem Statement:**  
Analyze the properties of radio waves from a station broadcasting at 98.6 MHz with 50 kW power.

###### Part a) Wavelength of radio waves

**Starting Formula:** $$c = f\lambda$$ (Wave relationship)

**Solution:**  
$$\lambda = \frac{c}{f} = \frac{3.00 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}}{98.6 \times 10^6 \text{ Hz}} = 3.04 \text{ m}$$

###### Part b) Number of photons emitted per second

**Starting Formulas:** 
- $$E = hf$$ (Energy of one photon)
- $$\text{Photons/second} = \frac{\text{Total Power}}{E_{\text{photon}}}$$

**Solution:**  
$$E_{\text{photon}} = h \times f = (6.63 \times 10^{-34} \,J \cdot s) \times (98.6 \times 10^6 \text{ Hz}) = 6.54 \times 10^{-27} \text{ J}$$

$$\text{Photons/second} = \frac{\text{Total Power}}{E_{\text{photon}}} = \frac{50 \times 10^3 \text{ W}}{6.54 \times 10^{-27} \text{ J}} = 7.65 \times 10^{29} \approx 7.6 \times 10^{29} \text{ photons/s}$$

###### Part c) Comparing radio and visible light photon energies

**Starting Formula:** $$\text{Ratio of energies} = \frac{E_{\text{radio}}}{E_{\text{visible}}}$$

**Solution:**  
Using a mid-range visible light frequency of about $$5.5 \times 10^{14} \text{ Hz}$$:  
$$E_{\text{visible}} = h \times f = (6.63 \times 10^{-34} \,J \cdot s) \times (5.5 \times 10^{14} \text{ Hz}) = 3.65 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}$$

$$\frac{E_{\text{radio}}}{E_{\text{visible}}} = \frac{6.54 \times 10^{-27} \text{ J}}{3.65 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}} \approx 1.79 \times 10^{-8} \approx 10^{-8}$$

A radio photon has approximately $$10^{-8}$$ times the energy of a visible photon (or about one hundred-millionth).

##### Problem 3: Double-Slit Experiment

**Problem Statement:**  
Calculate the distance between the central maximum and first bright fringe for light with wavelength 630 nm passing through slits separated by 0.300 mm, with a screen 2.0 m away.

**Starting Formulas:** 
- $$d\sin\theta = m\lambda$$ (Double-slit interference)
- $$y = L\tan\theta$$ (Position on screen)

**Solution:**  
For the first bright fringe, $$m = 1$$:  
$$\sin\theta = \frac{m\lambda}{d} = \frac{1 \cdot 630 \times 10^{-9} \text{ m}}{0.300 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}} = 2.1 \times 10^{-3}$$

For small angles, $$\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta \approx \theta$$ (in radians)

Distance on screen:  
$$y = L\tan\theta \approx L\sin\theta = (2.0 \text{ m}) \cdot (2.1 \times 10^{-3}) = 4.2 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m} = 4.2 \text{ mm}$$

##### Problem 4: De Broglie Wavelength

**Problem Statement:**  
Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of an electron with kinetic energy of 50 eV, and determine if it would show diffraction.

###### Part a) Calculating wavelength

**Starting Formulas:**
- $$E_K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$$ (Kinetic energy)
- $$\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{mv}$$ (De Broglie wavelength)

**Solution:**  
First, convert eV to Joules:  
$$E_K = 50 \text{ eV} \cdot 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J/eV} = 8.01 \times 10^{-18} \text{ J}$$

Find velocity from kinetic energy:  
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2E_K}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 8.01 \times 10^{-18} \text{ J}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31} \text{ kg}}} = \sqrt{1.76 \times 10^{13}} = 4.19 \times 10^6 \text{ m/s}$$

Calculate de Broglie wavelength:  
$$\lambda = \frac{h}{mv} = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \,J \cdot s}{(9.11 \times 10^{-31} \text{ kg}) \times (4.19 \times 10^6 \text{ m/s})} = 1.74 \times 10^{-10} \text{ m} \approx 0.17 \text{ nm}$$

###### Part b) Diffraction effects

**Analysis:**  
Since the electron's de Broglie wavelength (0.17 nm) is of the same order of magnitude as the opening size (1.0 nm), significant diffraction would occur.

For diffraction effects to be observable, the wavelength should be comparable to the obstacle or aperture size. With a ratio of approximately 1:6 (wavelength:opening), the electron would exhibit clear wave-like behavior and produce a noticeable diffraction pattern.

##### Problem 5: Photon Detection

**Problem Statement:**  
Determine if a detector can register green light (520 nm) with intensity $$1.0 \times 10^{-12} \text{ W/m²}$$.

###### Part a) Photons per second hitting detector

**Starting Formulas:**
- $$E_{\text{photon}} = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$ (Photon energy)
- $$\text{Photon flux} = \frac{\text{Intensity}}{E_{\text{photon}}}$$ (Photons per unit area)

**Solution:**  
Calculate energy of one photon:  
$$E_{\text{photon}} = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{(6.63 \times 10^{-34} \,J \cdot s) \times (3.00 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s})}{520 \times 10^{-9} \text{ m}} = 3.83 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}$$

Calculate photon flux:  
$$\text{Photon flux} = \frac{\text{Intensity}}{E_{\text{photon}}} = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-12} \text{ W/m²}}{3.83 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}} = 2.61 \times 10^6 \text{ photons/(s·m²)}$$

Total photons hitting detector:  
$$\text{Total rate} = \text{Photon flux} \times \text{Area} = (2.61 \times 10^6 \text{ photons/(s·m²)}) \times (1.0 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m²}) = 2.61 \text{ photons/s}$$

###### Part b) Detectability analysis

**Starting Formula:** $$\text{Photons per time interval} = \text{Rate} \times \text{Time interval}$$

**Solution:**  
Photons arriving in 1.0 ms:  
$$\text{Photons} = (2.61 \text{ photons/s}) \times (1.0 \times 10^{-3} \text{ s}) = 2.61 \times 10^{-3} \approx 0.003 \text{ photons}$$

Since less than 1 photon would arrive within the 1.0 ms interval, and the detector requires at least 100 photons in this interval to register a signal, this light would not be detectable with the given equipment.

##### Problem 6: Microwave Analysis

**Problem Statement:**  
Analyze the properties of microwaves in a 2.45 GHz microwave oven delivering 900 W of power.

###### Part a) Wavelength calculation

**Starting Formula:** $$c = f\lambda$$ (Wave relationship)

**Solution:**  
$$\lambda = \frac{c}{f} = \frac{3.00 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}}{2.45 \times 10^9 \text{ Hz}} = 0.122 \text{ m} = 12.2 \text{ cm}$$

###### Part b) Resonant absorption explanation

The 2.45 GHz frequency is specifically chosen because it matches a rotational resonance frequency of water molecules. Water molecules are polar, having a positive end and a negative end. When exposed to the alternating electric field of the microwave radiation, these dipoles continuously rotate to align with the oscillating field.

Since the field oscillates 2.45 billion times per second, the water molecules constantly rotate, generating friction and collisions with surrounding molecules. This molecular agitation transfers energy to neighboring molecules as heat.

This resonant absorption is much more efficient than non-resonant frequencies, allowing targeted heating of water-containing foods while allowing other materials (like ceramic and glass) to remain relatively cool.

###### Part c) Photon count calculation

**Starting Formulas:**
- $$E_{\text{photon}} = hf$$ (Photon energy)
- $$\text{Photons/second} = \frac{\text{Power}}{E_{\text{photon}}}$$

**Solution:**  
$$E_{\text{photon}} = h \times f = (6.63 \times 10^{-34} \,J \cdot s) \times (2.45 \times 10^9 \text{ Hz}) = 1.62 \times 10^{-24} \text{ J}$$

$$\text{Photons/second} = \frac{\text{Power}}{E_{\text{photon}}} = \frac{900 \text{ W}}{1.62 \times 10^{-24} \text{ J}} = 5.55 \times 10^{26} \text{ photons/s}$$

##### Problem 7: Human Eye Sensitivity

**Problem Statement:**  
Calculate the energy of 7 photons of green light (550 nm) and its thermal effect.

###### Part a) Energy carried by 7 photons

**Starting Formula:** $$E_{\text{total}} = 7 \cdot E_{\text{photon}} = 7 \cdot \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$

**Solution:**  
$$E_{\text{photon}} = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{(6.63 \times 10^{-34} \,J \cdot s) \times (3.00 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s})}{550 \times 10^{-9} \text{ m}} = 3.62 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}$$

$$E_{\text{total}} = 7 \cdot (3.62 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}) = 2.53 \times 10^{-18} \text{ J} \approx 2.52 \times 10^{-18} \text{ J}$$

###### Part b) Temperature increase calculation

**Starting Formula:** $$Q = mc\Delta T$$ (Heat transfer equation)

**Solution:**  
Rearranging for temperature change:  
$$\Delta T = \frac{Q}{mc} = \frac{E_{\text{total}}}{mc}$$

Using specific heat capacity of water $$c = 4186 J/(kg \cdot K)$$:  
$$\Delta T = \frac{2.52 \times 10^{-18} \text{ J}}{(1.0 \times 10^{-15} \text{ kg}) \times (4186 \, J/(kg \cdot K))} = \frac{2.52 \times 10^{-18}}{4.19 \times 10^{-12}} = 6.01 \times 10^{-7} \text{ K} \approx 6.0 \times 10^{-7} \text{ K}$$

This represents a negligible temperature increase, demonstrating that while the eye can detect this energy as light, its thermal effect is imperceptible.

##### Problem 8: X-ray Analysis

**Problem Statement:**  
Analyze the properties of X-rays used in medical imaging with wavelength of 0.1 nm.

###### Part a) Frequency and energy calculation

**Starting Formulas:**
- $$c = f\lambda$$ (Wave relationship)
- $$E = hf$$ (Photon energy)

**Solution:**  
Calculate frequency:  
$$f = \frac{c}{\lambda} = \frac{3.00 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}}{0.1 \times 10^{-9} \text{ m}} = 3.0 \times 10^{18} \text{ Hz}$$

Calculate energy:  
$$E = h \times f = (6.63 \times 10^{-34} \,J \cdot s) \times (3.0 \times 10^{18} \text{ Hz}) = 1.99 \times 10^{-15} \text{ J}$$

Converting to electron-volts:  
$$E \text{ in eV} = \frac{E \text{ in J}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J/eV}} = \frac{1.99 \times 10^{-15} \text{ J}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J/eV}} = 12,400 \text{ eV} = 12.4 \text{ keV}$$

###### Part b) Number of photons in 1.0 J

**Starting Formula:** $$\text{Number of photons} = \frac{\text{Total energy}}{E_{\text{photon}}}$$

**Solution:**  
$$\text{Number of photons} = \frac{1.0 \text{ J}}{1.99 \times 10^{-15} \text{ J}} = 5.03 \times 10^{14} \approx 5.0 \times 10^{14} \text{ photons}$$

###### Part c) Comparison with visible light

**Starting Formulas:** 
- $$E = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$ (Photon energy)
- $$\text{Ratio} = \frac{E_{\text{X-ray}}}{E_{\text{visible}}}$$

**Solution:**  
For visible light (550 nm):  
$$E_{\text{visible}} = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{(6.63 \times 10^{-34} \,J \cdot s) \times (3.00 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s})}{550 \times 10^{-9} \text{ m}} = 3.62 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}$$

Calculate the ratio:  
$$\frac{E_{\text{X-ray}}}{E_{\text{visible}}} = \frac{1.99 \times 10^{-15} \text{ J}}{3.62 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}} \approx 5.5 \times 10^{3} \approx 22,000$$

An X-ray photon has about 22,000 times more energy than a visible photon. This high energy means X-rays can ionize atoms and break molecular bonds, potentially damaging DNA and cellular structures. This explains why X-ray exposure must be limited and carefully controlled in medical settings, and why protective shielding is used for both patients and operators.

##### Problem 9: Electron Microscope Analysis

**Problem Statement:**  
Analyze the properties of electrons accelerated through a potential difference of 50 kV in an electron microscope.

###### Part a) Kinetic energy calculation

**Starting Formula:** $$E_K = qV$$ (Electric potential energy)

**Solution:**  
$$E_K = qV = (1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}) \times (50 \times 10^3 \text{ V}) = 8.01 \times 10^{-15} \text{ J} = 8.0 \times 10^{-15} \text{ J}$$

In electron-volts:  
$$E_K = 50 \text{ keV}$$

###### Part b) Momentum and de Broglie wavelength

**Starting Formulas:**
- $$E_K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$$ (Kinetic energy)
- $$p = mv$$ (Momentum)
- $$\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$$ (De Broglie wavelength)

**Solution:**  
Find velocity from kinetic energy:  
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2E_K}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 8.01 \times 10^{-15} \text{ J}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31} \text{ kg}}} = \sqrt{1.76 \times 10^{16}} = 1.33 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}$$

At this speed (approaching half the speed of light), relativistic effects become significant, but we'll use classical mechanics for an approximate answer.

Calculate momentum:  
$$p = mv = (9.11 \times 10^{-31} \text{ kg}) \times (1.33 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}) = 1.21 \times 10^{-22} \text{ kg·m/s} \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-22} \text{ kg·m/s}$$

Calculate de Broglie wavelength:  
$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \,J \cdot s}{1.21 \times 10^{-22} \text{ kg·m/s}} = 5.48 \times 10^{-12} \text{ m} = 5.5 \times 10^{-12} \text{ m} = 0.0055 \text{ nm}$$

###### Part c) Resolution comparison

Electron microscopes can achieve much higher resolution than light microscopes because the de Broglie wavelength of accelerated electrons (0.0055 nm at 50 keV) is much smaller than the wavelength of visible light (approximately 400-700 nm).

According to the Rayleigh criterion, the minimum resolvable feature size is approximately half the wavelength of the illumination source. With a wavelength roughly 100,000 times smaller than visible light, electron microscopes can theoretically resolve structures 100,000 times smaller, enabling imaging of individual atoms and molecular structures impossible with optical microscopes.

The wave-particle duality of electrons, as described by de Broglie, is directly applied in electron microscopy and represents one of the most important practical applications of quantum mechanics.

##### Problem 10: Photoelectric Effect Analysis

**Problem Statement:**  
Analyze the photoelectric effect when light with frequency 1.0 × 10¹⁵ Hz strikes a cesium surface with work function 1.9 eV.

###### Part a) Electron emission and maximum kinetic energy

**Starting Formulas:**
- $$E = hf$$ (Photon energy)
- $$KE_{max} = hf - \phi$$ (Photoelectric equation)

**Solution:**  
Calculate photon energy:  
$$E = hf = (6.63 \times 10^{-34} \,J \cdot s) \times (1.0 \times 10^{15} \text{ Hz}) = 6.63 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}$$

Convert to electron-volts:  
$$E \text{ in eV} = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J/eV}} = 4.14 \text{ eV} \approx 4.1 \text{ eV}$$

Since the photon energy (4.1 eV) exceeds the work function (1.9 eV), electrons will be emitted.

Calculate maximum kinetic energy:  
$$KE_{max} = hf - \phi = 4.1 \text{ eV} - 1.9 \text{ eV} = 2.2 \text{ eV}$$

###### Part b) Threshold frequency calculation

**Starting Formula:** $$hf_{threshold} = \phi$$ (Threshold condition)

**Solution:**  
$$f_{threshold} = \frac{\phi}{h} = \frac{1.9 \text{ eV} \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J/eV}}{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \,J \cdot s} = \frac{3.04 \times 10^{-19}}{6.63 \times 10^{-34}} = 4.59 \times 10^{14} \text{ Hz} \approx 4.6 \times 10^{14} \text{ Hz}$$

###### Part c) Effect of doubling intensity

When the intensity of light is doubled while maintaining the same frequency:

1. The maximum kinetic energy of emitted electrons remains unchanged at 2.2 eV.
   - This is because $$KE_{max}$$ depends only on the frequency of light (which determines the energy per photon) and the work function of the material.

2. The number of emitted electrons doubles.
   - This is because doubling the intensity means doubling the number of incident photons per unit time, which leads to twice as many electron emissions.

This result demonstrates a key principle of the photoelectric effect: the energy of emitted electrons depends on the frequency (not the intensity) of the incident light, while the number of emitted electrons depends on the intensity. This observation was crucial in establishing the particle nature of light, as it could not be explained by classical wave theory, which predicted that increasing intensity (amplitude of the wave) would increase the energy transferred to electrons.
