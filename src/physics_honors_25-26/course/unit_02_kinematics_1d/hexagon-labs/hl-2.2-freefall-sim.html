Hexagon Lab 2.2: Freefall Simulations

Complete the following investigations in your Science Notebook following the format of a scientific paper.

Abstract

This investigation uses a simulation to test the historical belief that heavier objects fall faster than lighter ones. By comparing the motion of objects in an environment with and without air resistance, we can isolate the effect of gravity and determine how mass influences the acceleration of an object in freefall, such as a coasting rocket after its engine has shut off.

Introduction

Background and Context

For nearly 2,000 years, Aristotle’s assertion that heavier objects fall faster than lighter ones was accepted as common sense truth. In the 17th century, Galileo Galilei challenged this idea with a powerful thought experiment: if a heavy cannonball and a light musket ball are dropped together, do they fall at the same rate? Answering this question is key to understanding the coasting phase of our sounding rocket’s flight, where it travels to its highest point under the influence of gravity alone. This investigation allows us to perform the definitive experiment in a controlled, simulated environment.

Research Question

Does an object’s mass affect its acceleration during freefall in a vacuum?

Dataset

This investigation uses an interactive simulation with the following features:

  • Two objects, a bowling ball ($m = 6.0 \text{ kg}$) and a feather ($m = 0.01 \text{ kg}$), can be dropped simultaneously from the same height.
  • A toggle switch allows for the presence of air resistance to be turned ON or OFF (simulating a vacuum).
  • The simulation visually displays the fall of both objects to the ground.

Investigation 2: Falling in a Vacuum (Without Air Resistance)

Variable Interpretation & Calibration

Now, we will replicate Galileo’s thought experiment by removing the variable of air resistance to see how gravity alone affects the two objects.

  3. In this idealized scenario, what is the only force acting on both objects?
