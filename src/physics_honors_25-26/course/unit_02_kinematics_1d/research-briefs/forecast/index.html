Unit 2 Research Brief: The Art of Observation

Launching a Sounding Rocket

NASA launches sounding rockets nearly every week from locations around the world. These suborbital research vehicles carry scientific instruments to altitudes between 50-1,500 km above Earth’s surface, far higher than weather balloons but not high enough to orbit like satellites. Unlike the dramatic launches of orbital rockets that we see on TV, sounding rockets follow a simpler trajectory: straight up, peak altitude, then straight back down.

But “simple” doesn’t mean easy to analyze. Mission control must track every second of the flight to ensure the scientific instruments deploy at the right time and altitude. The rocket’s velocity changes dramatically throughout the flight—accelerating rapidly during engine burn, then decelerating under gravity alone until it reaches its highest point (called apogee), and finally accelerating again as it falls back to Earth.

The Scenario: You are a mission analyst at NASA’s Sounding Rocket Program Office. Your team has been tasked with analyzing telemetry data from a recent launch to verify that the rocket performed as expected and reached its target apogee altitude.

Your Central Question:

How can we use velocity-time data from rocket telemetry to tell the complete story of a sounding rocket’s flight and predict its maximum altitude?

Your Task: The Forecast

Before you analyze the actual flight data in the lab, you need to make predictions about what you expect to see based on your understanding of motion. Mission analysts always start with theoretical predictions before examining real data—this helps them spot anomalies or unexpected behavior during flight.

Address the following points in a clear, well-organized entry:

Prediction 1: What do you predict the shape of a velocity-time graph will look like for a sounding rocket flight? Sketch your predicted graph and label the different phases you expect to see (engine burn, engine cutoff, ballistic flight).
 
Prediction 2: During which phase of flight do you predict the rocket will have its highest acceleration? Its lowest acceleration? Explain your reasoning using what you know about the rocket’s engine and gravity.
 
Prediction 3: If you know the rocket’s velocity at engine cutoff and the acceleration during ballistic flight, what kinematic equation could you use to predict when the rocket reaches apogee (maximum altitude)? Write the equation and identify each variable.
 
Thinking Lens: Patterns
Scientists look for patterns in data to understand underlying physical processes. What patterns do you predict you’ll see in the velocity data that will help you identify when the rocket engine turns on and off? How might these patterns be different from what you’d see for a car accelerating and then coasting to a stop? How might they be similar?

Next Steps: After completing your forecast, you’ll analyze real velocity-time data from a NASA sounding rocket launch in Hexagon Lab 2.1. Your predictions will help you recognize the key features in the actual data and understand what the rocket was doing at each moment of its flight.
