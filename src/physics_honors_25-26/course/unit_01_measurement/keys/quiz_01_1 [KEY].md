## Part A: Foundational Skills
### 1. Measurement and Conversion

**a) Convert $85.0 \text{ km/h}$ to $\text{m/s}$.**

The calculation requires converting kilometers to meters and hours to seconds. The initial value has three significant figures, so the final answer must also have three.

Setup:
$$85.0 \text{ km/h} \times \frac{1000 \text{ m}}{1 \text{ km}} \times \frac{1 \text{ h}}{3600 \text{ s}} = 23.6 \text{ m/s}$$

**Answer:** $23.6 \text{ m/s}$

**b) Calculate Luna's circumference.**

First, determine what fraction of a full circle the angle represents. Then, set up a proportion where that fraction equals the ratio of the arc length (distance between stations) to the total circumference.

Setup:
$$\text{Fraction} = \frac{360°}{6.0°} = \frac{60}{1}$$

$$\frac{60}{1} = \frac{\text{Circumference}}{450 \text{ km}}$$

$$\text{Circumference} = 60 \times 450 \text{ km}$$

**Answer (with two significant figures):** $27000 \text{ km}$ or $2.7 \times 10^4 \text{ km}$

### 2. Vector Components

For a vector $\vec{d} = 60.0 \text{ m}$ at $30.0°$ north of east:

**a) Sketch the vector and its components.**

A correct sketch shows:
- A coordinate system with x-y axes (or E-N).
- The vector $\vec{d}$ drawn in the first quadrant.
- The $30.0°$ angle clearly marked between the vector and the positive x-axis (East).
- The components $d_x$ and $d_y$ drawn as the legs of a right triangle, with $\vec{d}$ as the hypotenuse.

**b) Calculate the eastward component ($d_x$).**

Setup:
$$\cos(\theta) = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} = \frac{d_x}{d}$$

$$d_x = d \cos(\theta) = (60.0 \text{ m})\cos(30.0°)$$

**Answer (with three significant figures):** $d_x = 52.0 \text{ m}$

**c) Calculate the northward component ($d_y$).**

Setup:
$$\sin(\theta) = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}} = \frac{d_y}{d}$$

$$d_y = d \sin(\theta) = (60.0 \text{ m})\sin(30.0°)$$

**Answer (with three significant figures):** $d_y = 30.0 \text{ m}$

## Part B: Application and Inquiry
### 3. Scientific Modeling

For a desk at coordinates $(4.2 \text{ m}, 3.0 \text{ m})$:

**a) Determine the displacement vector $\vec{d}$.**

The magnitude is found using the Pythagorean theorem. The direction is found using the inverse tangent function. The inputs have two significant figures.

Magnitude Setup:
$$|\vec{d}| = \sqrt{d_x^2 + d_y^2} = \sqrt{(4.2 \text{ m})^2 + (3.0 \text{ m})^2} = \sqrt{17.64 \text{ m}^2 + 9.0 \text{ m}^2} = \sqrt{26.64 \text{ m}^2}$$

**Magnitude Answer (two sig figs):** $5.2 \text{ m}$

Direction Setup:
$$\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{d_y}{d_x}\right) = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{3.0 \text{ m}}{4.2 \text{ m}}\right)$$

**Direction Answer (two sig figs):** $36°$

**Final Vector:** $\vec{d} = 5.2 \text{ m}$ at $36°$ from the origin (or specified axis)

**b) Why is a mathematical model more reliable than a sketch?**

A mathematical coordinate system is more reliable than a hand-drawn sketch because it is objective, precise, and scalable. Unlike a sketch, which is subjective and prone to error, a coordinate system provides unambiguous, reproducible measurements that can be used for precise calculations and easily scaled to model larger areas.

### 4. Cross-Cutting Concepts

**a) Why are vector components essential for GPS precision?**

Vector components are essential for global precision because they create a consistent grid system (latitude and longitude) that is independent of any single origin point. Using a magnitude and direction would require a different vector for every user relative to a single, arbitrary spot on Earth, which is computationally impractical and less accurate for describing small changes in position over a curved surface.

**b) What pattern connects Eratosthenes's method to GPS?**

The fundamental pattern connecting both is the use of triangulation (or trilateration) from known reference points. Eratosthenes used one known distance on Earth and the sun's known parallel rays as references to calculate Earth's circumference. Similarly, GPS uses signals from multiple satellites (whose positions are known references) to triangulate a user's unknown position on Earth based on signal travel time.
