# Checkpoint 4: Interpreting Negative Force {#checkpoint-4-interpreting-negative-force}

In the data, we noticed that the force measured on the green cart is negative while the force measured on the blue cart is positive.

**What does a negative speed mean?** 

**Velocity vs. Speed Reminder**
As we've discussed before, **velocity** is the directional speed of an object.

It is a vector quantity with:
- Magnitude (size)
- Direction

Opposite directions are assigned opposite signs:
- Negative velocity = moving to the left
- Positive velocity = moving to the right

For example, if a cart moves at 5 m/s for 2 seconds:

$$\text{Distance} = \text{Velocity} \times \text{Time} = 5 \text{ m/s} \times 2 \text{ s} = 10 \text{ m}$$
**What does a negative force mean?**

**Force as a Vector Quantity**
Force is a vector quantity; it has:
- Magnitude (size)
- Direction

The **magnitude** is the absolute value of a quantity (ignoring its direction).

Forces acting in opposite directions are assigned opposite signs:
- Negative force = push to the left
- Positive force = push to the right

## Mathematical Relationships {#math-1}

Let's examine the precise mathematical relationship between mass and velocity changes in our collisions.

For Collision E (where the green cart has double mass):
- Initial velocity (green): 0.79 m/s
- Final velocity (green): 0.33 m/s
- Initial velocity (blue): 0 m/s
- Final velocity (blue): 0.92 m/s

For Collision F (where the blue cart has double mass):
- Initial velocity (green): 0.8 m/s
- Final velocity (green): -0.12 m/s
- Initial velocity (blue): 0 m/s
- Final velocity (blue): 0.46 m/s

**Mathematical Relationship**
The data reveals that:

$$\frac{m_1}{m_2} = -\frac{\Delta v_2}{\Delta v_1}$$

In other words, the ratio of masses equals the negative reciprocal of the ratio of velocity changes.

This means when one object has twice the mass of another, its velocity change will be half as large (and in the opposite direction).

**Example Calculation**
Let's calculate the velocity changes for Collision E:

For the green cart (double mass):
$$\Delta v_{\text{green}} = v_{\text{final}} - v_{\text{initial}} = 0.33 \text{ m/s} - 0.79 \text{ m/s} = -0.46 \text{ m/s}$$

For the blue cart (normal mass):
$$\Delta v_{\text{blue}} = v_{\text{final}} - v_{\text{initial}} = 0.92 \text{ m/s} - 0 \text{ m/s} = 0.92 \text{ m/s}$$

Ratio of velocity changes:
$$\frac{\Delta v_{\text{green}}}{\Delta v_{\text{blue}}} = \frac{-0.46 \text{ m/s}}{0.92 \text{ m/s}} = -0.5$$

Ratio of masses:
$$\frac{m_{\text{green}}}{m_{\text{blue}}} = \frac{2m}{m} = 2$$

Checking our relationship:
$$\frac{m_{\text{green}}}{m_{\text{blue}}} = -\frac{\Delta v_{\text{blue}}}{\Delta v_{\text{green}}}$$
$$2 = -\frac{0.92}{-0.46} = 2$$

The relationship is verified!

# Checkpoint 9: Real-World Applications {#checkpoint-9-real-world-applications}

Let's apply our mathematical relationship to real-world situations.

**A large truck (20,000 kg) collides with a stationary shopping cart (20 kg). How will their velocity changes compare?**

Using our relationship $\frac{m_1}{m_2} = -\frac{\Delta v_2}{\Delta v_1}$, calculate:
1. The ratio of masses between the truck and shopping cart
2. The ratio of velocity changes you would expect
3. If the truck's velocity decreases by 0.01 m/s, what would the shopping cart's velocity change be?

**Solution:**
1. Ratio of masses:
   $$\frac{m_{\text{truck}}}{m_{\text{cart}}} = \frac{20,000 \text{ kg}}{20 \text{ kg}} = 1,000$$

2. Expected ratio of velocity changes:
   $$\frac{m_{\text{truck}}}{m_{\text{cart}}} = -\frac{\Delta v_{\text{cart}}}{\Delta v_{\text{truck}}}$$
   $$1,000 = -\frac{\Delta v_{\text{cart}}}{\Delta v_{\text{truck}}}$$
   $$\frac{\Delta v_{\text{cart}}}{\Delta v_{\text{truck}}} = -1,000$$

3. If truck's velocity decreases by 0.01 m/s:
   $$\Delta v_{\text{truck}} = -0.01 \text{ m/s}$$
   $$\frac{\Delta v_{\text{cart}}}{-0.01 \text{ m/s}} = -1,000$$
   $$\Delta v_{\text{cart}} = -0.01 \text{ m/s} \times (-1,000) = 10 \text{ m/s}$$
   
   The shopping cart's velocity would increase by 10 m/s (about 22 mph)!

This helps explain why in collisions between vehicles of very different masses (like a truck and a car), the smaller vehicle experiences much more dramatic changes in velocity—which directly relates to safety outcomes for the occupants.
