## The Particle Model of Electromagnetism

|Wave Model|Particle Model
|-|-|
|| ![EM variables in the particle model](../../../assets/images/p5_waves/p5l10_particle_vars.png){: style="width: 50%"} |

To explain the phenomena that the wave model could not, scientists developed a new model: the particle model. This model proposes that electromagnetic radiation is not a continuous wave, but rather a stream of tiny packets of energy called photons.

In the particle model, each photon carries a specific amount of energy that is proportional to its frequency. This means that higher frequency photons, such as ultraviolet light photons, have more energy than lower frequency photons, such as infrared light photons.

**Photons** are particles of light, imgaginable as *discrete* packets of energy. The energy of a photon is directly proportional to its frequency: higher frequency photons have more energy.

**Amplitude:** In the particle model, the amplitude of electromagnetic radiation corresponds to the number of photons. A high-amplitude wave means more photons, while a low-amplitude wave means fewer photons.

## Explaining the Photoelectric Effect

The particle model provides a natural explanation for the photoelectric effect. When a photon strikes a metal surface, it can transfer its energy to an electron. If the photon's energy is greater than the binding energy of the electron (the energy holding the electron in the metal), the electron is ejected.

![alt text](../../../assets/images/p5_waves/photoelectric_effect.png){: class="figure-right"}

The energy of the ejected electron depends on the energy (frequency) of the photon, not the number (intensity) of photons. There is a threshold frequency because the photons must have enough energy to overcome the binding energy of the electrons in the metal. This explanation perfectly matches the experimental observations of the photoelectric effect, which the wave model could not account for.

### The Energy of Photons and Cancer Risk

The particle model also explains why high-frequency electromagnetic radiation, such as ultraviolet (UV) light, is more likely to cause cancer than low-frequency radiation, such as infrared (IR) light.

High-frequency photons carry more energy than low-frequency photons. When a UV photon interacts with a DNA molecule, it can transfer enough energy to break chemical bonds and damage the DNA, potentially leading to mutations and cancer. IR photons, on the other hand, do not carry enough energy to cause this kind of damage.
