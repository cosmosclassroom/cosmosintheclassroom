# Amplitude/Frequency Evidence

### Do EM radiation’s frequency and/or amplitude affect the risk of harm?

Humans create EM radiation in a wide variety of frequencies and amplitudes. Medical research can help us learn more about the impact of various types of EM radiation on humans.

* What are the health benefits of exposure to radiation of specific frequencies and amplitudes?  
* What patterns can we find in the evidence in this reading that help us learn more about the health risks of certain types of radiation?

### INFRARED

Some health spas offer exposure to infrared (IR) light in rooms called saunas. These saunas are places where guests can sit and soak in the radiation, which they experience as heat. A 2015 study suggests that this kind of IR light can break down skin’s collagen, which can in turn contribute to wrinkles. However, IR amplitudes in this study were very high--much higher than the exposure we would expect if someone spent an hour in a sauna. Low-amplitude IR is common. IR is emitted from heat lamps, space heaters, electric stoves, and even the Sun. Low-amplitude IR exposure has been shown to help wounds heal and to reduce swelling. It can also help treat acne. Furthermore, a 2020 study found that “infrared light does not cause sunburn and is not known to cause skin cancer.”

### VISIBLE LIGHT: 

Some people are affected by a type of depression that is caused by a lack of sunlight. This is called Seasonal Affective Disorder, or SAD. Current studies show that visible light therapy can treat SAD symptoms. Some professionals recommend that SAD patients use "SAD lamps." SAD lamps are extremely bright lights designed to simulate sunlight. SAD lights are not considered to contribute to the risk of skin cancer because they typically have filters that block most ultraviolet light. Some evidence shows that the brightest SAD lamps can cause skin redness in people with very sensitive skin, but this is not common. Visible light is not dangerous at lower amplitudes and has not been shown to have any negative health effects.

### ULTRAVIOLET:

Ultraviolet (UV) radiation is a type of ionizing radiation, meaning it can eject electrons from atoms. Fairly low amplitudes of UV are used by beauticians to harden gel nail polish. Police detectives may also use low amplitudes of UV to make certain bodily fluids glow when investigating a crime scene. Very low amplitudes of UV are used to harden resin in some very precise 3D printers. Some beauty companies advertise “tanning beds,” which can make people’s skin darker over time by exposing them to high amplitudes of UV. This is no different from a suntan or a sunburn. Healthy human skin naturally varies in pigment, but large changes in color due to UV exposure tend to be a sign of skin damage, not skin health.  
Even at high amplitudes, UV cannot transmit completely through human skin. Research clearly shows that UV contributes to skin cancer and eyelid cancer, but other forms of cancer deeper in the body cannot be connected to UV. Even low-amplitude UV exposure may increase risk of skin cancer. This risk increases with the amount of time spent in the sun. To be safe, it's best to use "broad-spectrum" sunscreen, wear sun-safe clothing, hats, and eyewear, and limit the time you are in the sun unprotected.

### X-RAYS:

X-rays are used in medical imaging to help get a better idea of what is inside someone’s body. Unlike ultraviolet rays, X-rays can transmit through skin and organ cells. X-rays can then get absorbed by bones and other dense matter. This absorption can be harmful to humans, even at low amplitudes. Patients don’t get much exposure from one scan, but studies among workers who operated X-ray machines before 1980 show that exposure to many scans can contribute significantly to cancer risk. *CT scans* and *nuclear imaging* enable professionals to analyze details about human organs that are otherwise very hard to learn about. Both of those scans require exposure to high-amplitude X-rays. To avoid repeated exposure, scans like these are only used when absolutely necessary.
