Hexagon Lab 1.1B: From Power Strip to Power Grid

Today’s Question: How does electricity travel from a power plant to your phone charger?

What You Need:

  • Your Science Notebook
  • Calculator
  • Colored pencils or markers

Background:

You’ve learned Ohm’s Law and can calculate voltage and current in simple circuits. But how does electricity actually get from a power plant hundreds of miles away to the outlet in your classroom? Today you’ll trace this journey step by step.

Part 1: The Power Strip Investigation

Data Set A: Power Strip Anatomy

Here’s what you found when we took apart the power strip:

Component What It Does Connected To
Plug (male) Gets electricity from wall outlet Wall outlet
Internal wires Carry electricity through the strip Multiple outlets
Circuit breaker Stops electricity if too much flows Internal wires
Outlets (female) Provide electricity to devices Your phone charger

Step 1: In your notebook, draw the path electricity takes from the wall outlet to your phone charger. Use arrows to show direction.

Step 2: Answer these questions:

  1. Are the outlets in the power strip connected in series or parallel? How do you know?
  2. If one outlet breaks, will the others still work? Explain using your circuit knowledge.

Part 2: Scaling Up - From Outlet to Building

Data Set B: Classroom Electrical System

Your teacher measured the voltage at different points in our school’s electrical system:

Location Voltage Current Capacity
Wall outlet (where we plug in) 120 V 15 A
Classroom breaker box 120 V 15 A per circuit
Main school panel 240 V 200 A total
Transformer outside school 7,200 V High

Step 3: Graph this data. Put “Location” on the x-axis and “Voltage” on the y-axis.

Step 4: What pattern do you notice as you go from the outlet toward the transformer?

Make a Claim: Complete this sentence: “As electricity travels from the transformer to the outlet, the voltage _______.” Cite specific numbers from the data table as evidence.

Part 3: The Big Picture - Regional Power Grid

Data Set C: Texas Power Grid Components

Here are the major parts of the Texas power grid and their typical voltages:

Grid Component Voltage Level Purpose
Power plants 13,000 V Generate electricity
Transmission lines (long distance) 345,000 V Carry power across Texas
Distribution substations 7,200 V Reduce voltage for neighborhoods
Local transformers 240 V Final voltage reduction
Your house 120 V Safe voltage for appliances

Step 5: Create a flow chart showing how electricity moves from power plant to your house. Include all five components and their voltage levels.

Step 6: Why do you think the transmission lines use such high voltage (345,000 V) when your house only needs 120 V?

Hint: Think about what you learned in Problem Set 1.1 about power losses.

Part 4: Connecting to the Texas Crisis

Data Set D: What Happened in February 2021

During the Texas blackout, different parts of the grid failed:

Day Power Plants Working Transmission Lines Distribution Houses with Power
Feb 13 95% 100% 100% 95%
Feb 15 60% 90% 85% 40%
Feb 17 45% 75% 60% 30%
Feb 19 70% 95% 90% 65%

Step 7: Which part of the grid failed first? Which failed last?

Step 8: Explain why transmission and distribution problems got worse even after some power plants came back online.

Part 5: Check Your Understanding

Complete these sentences using evidence from today’s investigation:

  1. “Electricity travels from power plants to homes by _______.”

  2. “Transmission lines use very high voltage because _______.”

  3. “The Texas blackout got worse because _______.”

  4. “Power grids are like systems because they have _______.”
