Slide 4: Energy Source Reliability

Energy Source Reliability

Not all energy sources respond the same way to extreme conditions.

Definition: Reliability = ability to provide power when needed, especially during emergencies

Key Insight: Each source has different strengths and vulnerabilities

Slide 5: Natural Gas Performance

Natural Gas (40% of Texas Power)

Normal Operation: Cheap, fast startup, very reliable During February 2021: Dropped from 40% to 20% of grid supply

Why it failed: Gas wells and pipelines froze in extreme cold Lesson: “Reliable” depends on the conditions

Slide 6: Wind Power Performance

Wind Power (20% of Texas Power)

Normal Operation: Clean, cheap to run, weather-dependent During February 2021: Dropped from 20% to 12% of grid supply

Why it struggled: Turbine blades iced over, reduced wind speeds Surprise: Performed better than expected for the conditions

Slide 7: Coal and Nuclear Performance

Coal and Nuclear Power

Coal (15% of Texas Power): Stayed at 15% - kept running Nuclear (10% of Texas Power): Stayed at 10% - kept running

Why they didn’t fail: Designed for continuous operation, less weather-sensitive Trade-off: Higher environmental impact (coal) or safety concerns (nuclear)

Slide 8: The Storage Challenge

Why Can’t We Store Electricity?

The Problem: Electricity must be used the instant it’s made Analogy: Like a river - water flows constantly, can’t “pause” the flow

Storage Reality: Converting electricity to other forms (chemical, mechanical) loses energy and costs money

Slide 9: Real-Time Balancing

Supply Must Equal Demand Every Second

Physics Requirement: If supply < demand, voltage drops and system fails Grid Challenge: Predict and match electricity use 24/7 Texas Crisis: When supply dropped 40%, demand was still 100%

Formula: Grid Stability = Supply - Demand = 0 (always)

Slide 10: ANALYSIS ACTIVITY

THINK-PAIR-SHARE: Design Challenge

Individual (2 minutes): You’re designing energy for a new city. Rank these priorities 1-5:

  • Lowest cost
  • Least environmental impact
  • Most reliable during emergencies
  • Fastest to build
  • Creates most local jobs

Pair (3 minutes): Compare rankings with partner. Did you agree? Why or why not?

Share (2 minutes): What was hardest about ranking these priorities?

Slide 11: Equity in Energy Access

Who Loses Power First?

Data from Houston (Feb 2021):

  • Wealthy areas: 2 days without power
  • Middle-class areas: 4 days without power
  • Low-income areas: 7 days without power

Cause: Older infrastructure in low-income neighborhoods fails first

Slide 12: Policy Trade-offs

Engineering vs. Policy Decisions

Engineers ask: What’s technically possible? Policymakers ask: What’s politically and economically acceptable?

Example: Winterizing Texas grid would cost $10 billion but prevent future blackouts Trade-off: Higher electricity bills vs. lower blackout risk

Slide 13: Criteria and Constraints

Design Criteria (What we want):

  • Reliable power supply
  • Affordable electricity
  • Clean environment
  • Energy security

Design Constraints (What limits us):

  • Available money
  • Existing infrastructure
  • Political support
  • Technical capabilities

Slide 14: Thinking Lens

Thinking Lens: Cause and Effect

How can the failure of one energy source cause cascading effects throughout the entire system and community?

Example: Gas plants fail → Less supply → Voltage drops → More plants shut down for safety → Even less supply

Slide 16: Summary

Summary: Answering Our Questions

How do different energy sources perform during extreme weather? Each source has unique vulnerabilities - gas freezes, wind ices, but coal/nuclear stay stable

Why can’t we easily store electricity for emergencies? Electricity must be used instantly; storage requires expensive energy conversion

How do policy decisions affect which communities lose power first? Infrastructure investment decisions determine which neighborhoods have reliable equipment
