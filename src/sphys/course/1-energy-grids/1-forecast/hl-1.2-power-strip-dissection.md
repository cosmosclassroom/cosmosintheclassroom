Pre-Lab | Power Strip Dissection
In this lab, you will explore basic electrical circuits to understand how energy flows and how larger power grids work. Using a D-battery, alligator clips, a power strip, and a small light bulb, you’ll construct simple circuits and investigate how they behave. Two types of circuits you’ll encounter are series circuits and parallel circuits.

In a series circuit, all components are connected in a single path, so the same current flows through each component. If one part of the circuit is disconnected or a component fails, the entire circuit stops working. An example is a string of old holiday lights—if one bulb burns out, the whole string goes dark. 

In a parallel circuit, the components are connected along multiple paths, so the current can flow through more than one route. If one component fails, the others can continue to function. Most household wiring uses parallel circuits, which is why one light going out doesn't cause the entire house to lose power.

This lab should be completed in your Science Notebook.

1. What happens in a series circuit if one component, like a light bulb, is removed or stops working? How does this affect the flow of electricity?


 
2. In a parallel circuit, how does the flow of electricity differ compared to a series circuit? What advantage does this provide in real-world applications, such as household wiring?



 
3. How does the on-off switch in the power strip control the circuit, and how does this relate to how power grids manage electricity distribution to homes and businesses?

Hexagon Lab 1.1: Building the First Circuit Model
Introduction
How can we possibly begin to understand a system as vast as a power grid? The same way scientists and engineers always do: by starting with a simple model. A model isn't just a smaller copy; it's a tool for thinking. It strips away the overwhelming complexity of the real world to help us focus on the fundamental relationships at play. Our journey begins not with a power plant, but with a battery, a bulb, and a few wires.
Materials
1 D-Cell Battery
1 Small Lightbulb (e.g., a flashlight bulb)
2 Wires with alligator clips
The Challenge
Using only the materials provided, your task is to make the bulb light up. But the goal isn't just to get a result—it's to develop a model that explains how and why it works.
The Investigation
Explore and Sketch: Discover as many different arrangements as you can that successfully light the bulb. In your notebook, sketch each successful arrangement.
Document Failures: Find a few arrangements that do not work. Sketch these as well.
Find the Pattern: Look at all of your sketches.
What do all the successful circuits have in common?
What do all the failed attempts have in common?
Develop a Rule: Based on your observations, write a single sentence that serves as a "rule" for making a bulb light up. This rule is the first version of your circuit model.
Making Your Thinking Visible
Draw Your Model: Choose your most reliable arrangement. Draw a clean, clear diagram of it in your research log.
Show the Flow: On your diagram, use arrows to show the path you believe the energy is taking to get from the battery to the bulb and back.
Explain Your Model: Below your diagram, write a short caption that explains why your arrangement works, using the "rule" you developed in the investigation.
This simple model of a closed loop is the fundamental building block for every electrical system, from the phone in your pocket to the power grid that spans a continent.
