# Reclaiming Educational Greatness: A Case for Classical Learning

The democratization of education represented one of humanity's greatest achievements, opening the doors of learning to all students regardless of background or station. But somewhere in our well-intentioned efforts to make education accessible, we made a tragic miscalculation. Instead of asking "How do we help all students engage with Plato, Shakespeare, and Euclid?" we began asking "Are these works too difficult for most students?" This subtle shift in questioning led us away from our fundamental mission: introducing young minds to the best that human civilization has produced. We replaced intellectual challenge with therapeutic comfort, mistaking difficulty for harm rather than recognizing it as the very mechanism through which minds grow stronger.

Our educational reforms, driven by genuine concern for student welfare, inadvertently diminished our expectations of what students could achieve. We embraced theories about developmental appropriateness and relevance that, while containing wisdom, were applied so broadly that they became barriers to greatness. We convinced ourselves that students needed material that immediately connected to their lived experience, forgetting that encountering the foreign, the challenging, and the profound has always been education's greatest gift. A sixteen-year-old doesn't need to see themselves reflected in every text to be moved by the Allegory of the Cave or inspired by the elegance of geometric proof. They need to discover that their minds are capable of grappling with ideas that have challenged and elevated human thinking for centuries.

Classical education offers students something our contemporary approaches too often deny them: the assumption that they are capable of greatness: of grappling with great thoughts and exploring great ideas. When we place rigorous texts and demanding ideas before students, we communicate our belief in their intellectual capacity. When we ask them to wrestle with Shakespeare's language or follow Euclid's reasoning, we invite them into a conversation with the finest minds in human history. This is not about nostalgia for an imagined golden age, but about recognizing that some ideas, some works, some approaches to understanding have proven their worth across centuries and cultures. Our students deserve nothing less than this intellectual inheritance, and they are far more capable of claiming it than our diminished expectations suggest.
