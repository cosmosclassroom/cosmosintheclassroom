About Cosmos in the Classroom

School systems have a responsibility to guarantee a viable, aligned curriculum. For decades, the best practical solution was a standardized, pre-packaged resource. But "one-size-fits-none-well" solutions have a practical downside: they risk turning curriculum and content experts into script-readers. This fuels burnout and resentment. We believe there's a better way.

The Origin Story

A Veteran Colleague: The Knowledge We're Losing

I have a colleague. A veteran biology teacher.

She taught in a jungle school. Got caught up in that experience—literally caught up, the way life sometimes just happens to you. Came back. Started teaching here.

And she has stories. Field stories. Not textbook biology. Lived biology. The kind of understanding you can't get from a curriculum guide.

She's part of our school's history now. Part of our memetic identity, if you want to get technical about it. When students take her class, they're not just learning biology—they're inheriting a piece of that jungle school. That field experience. That wealth.

And when she retires? Gone.

Oh, sure, someone else will teach biology. They'll use the textbook. They'll hit the standards. But that specific wisdom? The way she connects rainforest ecology to evolutionary biology to human behavior because she lived it?

That evaporates.

And the thing is—we know this is happening. We know this biologist has something irreplaceable. Just like we know another colleague down the hall has that primate social behavior unit. Just like we know every veteran teacher in this building has their own unique contribution to our collective knowledge.

But we don't preserve it. We don't even try. Not because we don't care. But because we're too busy with the goddamn paperwork.

A Social Studies Teacher: The Creativity We're Crushing

I have another colleague. A social studies teacher.

And I showed him something. How to generate an image of 1700s New York using AI. Just... playing around, really. Experimenting.

And we started talking. About his favorite authors. About how he'd love to design a unit inspired by them but never has time because the curriculum is locked down and there's pacing guides and—

And I showed him. Right there. How he could use AI to draft it. Not to replace his thinking—to support it. To handle the scaffolding while he focused on the design. The pedagogy. The experience he wanted students to have.

And his face lit up.

I don't mean he smiled. I mean I watched creativity flood back into his face. Like a river that had been dammed suddenly breaking through.

He started talking faster. Ideas tumbling out. "What if we did this? And this? And we could connect it to—"

That. THAT. That's what I want back. For all of us.

A computer can't do that. A computer can't be this teacher getting excited about teaching again. Can't be the moment when a teacher remembers why they went into this profession in the first place.

But a computer can handle the compliance paperwork. The standards alignment. The documentation that nobody reads but everybody's required to produce.

And if that frees up this teacher to be creative again? If that lets our veteran biologist capture her jungle school wisdom before she retires?

Then maybe—maybe—we're using these tools the right way.

Our Philosophy: The Human Work & The Machine Work

For all good reasons, master teachers modified their "prix-fixe" content. They adapted for differentiation, relevance, style, and complexity of task.

This "fraying" of the standardized curriculum isn't a bug—it's a feature. It is the natural, necessary consequence of a static curriculum being used in a dynamic ecosystem that requires human adaptation.

We believe that existing curriculum is the foundational asset upon which real teaching can be sculpted by master practitioners—with machine assistance to handle the digital tedium.

The Machine Work

Computers excel at computational, repetitive tasks:

  • Cross-referencing standards across hundreds of documents
  • Maintaining perfect institutional memory (the "Changelog")
  • Generating multiple versions of content for differentiation
  • Checking policy alignment against mission statements
  • Recalling specific examples from years of curriculum materials

Let them do this work. They're better at it than we are.

The Human Work

Humans excel at creative, relational, judgment-based work:

  • Designing transformative learning experiences
  • Exercising pedagogical judgment and creativity
  • Building meaningful relationships with students
  • Making strategic decisions about curriculum direction
  • Inspiring intellectual curiosity and wonder

This is the work only humans can do. This is what teaching is.

When I sat with that social studies teacher, watching his face light up—that's what this looks like.
A human remembering what it feels like to be human, freed from the machine work.

Our Non-Negotiable Commitment: Privacy First

This isn't about surveillance. It's about support.

We Never Store Student PII

Our process analyzes curriculum documents, standards, and instructional materials. We never collect, store, or process student personally identifiable information.

We Never Evaluate Teachers

The Codex is a support tool for curriculum teams and teachers—never a surveillance or evaluation system. It serves educators; it doesn't monitor them.

Your Data Stays Yours

Your curriculum DNA, institutional knowledge, and custom Codex configurations belong to your district. We build the tools; you own the intellectual property.

We're educators first. We understand the ethical implications of AI in schools because we live them. Privacy and teacher autonomy aren't features—they're foundational principles.
