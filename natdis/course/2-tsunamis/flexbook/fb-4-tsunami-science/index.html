title: ‘Chapter 4: The Scientist: The Hydrodynamics of Disaster’ layout: flexbook date: ‘2024-10-15’ course: Natural Disasters unit: ‘2’ chapter: ‘4’ content_type: reference Chapter 4: The Scientist: The Hydrodynamics of Disaster (Unit 2: Tsunamis)

The Central Question: How does a brief, massive shift in the Earth’s crust transform into a wall of water that can cross an entire ocean, and how do we measure its speed and energy?

The Narrative Arc You have spent Unit 1 studying the solid Earth—the forces of plate tectonics, the rigid response of buildings, and the economic constraints of a community rooted in place. Now, we shift our focus to the fluid Earth. Tsunamis are perhaps the most deceptive of all natural hazards, born from the violence of an earthquake but traveling as a peaceful, almost undetectable wave across the deep ocean.

Today, you return to the role of the Scientist to understand the hydrodynamics—the physics of fluids in motion—that governs these destructive waves. Our journey moves from the epicenter of a seismic event to the shallow waters of the coastline, revealing the hidden mathematics that turns a ripple into a catastrophe.

4.1 The Birth of a Tsunami: Vertical Displacement Unlike wind-generated waves that disturb only the ocean’s surface, a tsunami is a full-column wave. This means the wave energy disturbs the entire depth of the ocean, from the seafloor to the surface.

Tsunamis are most often generated by a massive, sudden vertical movement of the seafloor—a movement known as seismic slip.

Subduction Zones: The most common source is a megathrust earthquake in a subduction zone, where one tectonic plate is forced beneath another. When the two plates lock and then suddenly release, the overriding plate snaps upward, displacing billions of tons of water instantaneously.

Landslides and Volcanoes: Less commonly, tsunamis can be caused by massive undersea landslides or volcanic collapse, but the largest and most destructive are born from seismic events.

The key scientific difference lies in the wavelength: wind waves have a short wavelength (tens of meters); tsunamis have an enormous wavelength (hundreds of kilometers). This explains why a tsunami feels like a rapid rise in sea level rather than a classic breaking wave until it reaches the shore.

4.2 The Physics of Speed: The Shallow Water Wave Formula To track and predict a tsunami’s arrival time, scientists must calculate its speed precisely. In the deep ocean, the speed of a tsunami is determined almost entirely by the depth of the water (d), not the energy of the earthquake that caused it. Therefore, tsunamis are mathematically treated as shallow water waves, even when traveling over deep ocean trenches.

The crucial formula for determining the speed of a tsunami (v) is:

v= g⋅d ​

Where:

v is the wave speed (in meters per second, m/s).

g is the acceleration due to gravity (a constant, approximately 9.8 m/s 2 ).

d is the depth of the ocean (in meters, m).

Implications:

Deep Ocean Speed: In the deep ocean (depth ≈4,000 m), a tsunami can travel at over 200 m/s (about 720 km/h or 450 mph), roughly the speed of a jet airliner.

Near-Shore Slowdown: As the wave approaches the shore and the depth (d) rapidly decreases, the wave speed (v) decreases dramatically.

Height Increase: Because the total energy is conserved (the wave slows down but the water behind it continues to press forward), the wave’s height must increase—a process known as shoaling. This is why a barely noticeable wave in the deep ocean becomes a destructive wall near the coast.
