▶ Key Vocabulary (3 terms)
Tsunami

A series of large ocean waves, typically caused by underwater earthquakes or volcanic eruptions.

Wavelength

The distance between corresponding points of two consecutive waves.

Shoaling

The effect by which surface waves entering shallower water change in wave height (they slow down, shorten in wavelength, and grow much taller).

▶ Key Formulae (2 formulas)

$Speed_{wave} \approx \sqrt{g \cdot d}$

Shallow Water Wave Speed: For a tsunami (which has a wavelength so long the entire ocean is “shallow” to it), the speed is determined not by the wave itself, but by the ocean depth ($d$) and the acceleration due to gravity ($g \approx 9.8 \, m/s^2$).

$Travel \, Time = \frac{Distance}{Speed}$

Travel Time: This fundamental relationship allows scientists to forecast a tsunami’s arrival time by dividing the total distance it must travel by its calculated speed.

Introduction: The Silent Surge

In the deep ocean, a tsunami can pass completely unnoticed. It is not a towering wall of water, but a subtle surge of energy, often less than a meter high, moving at the speed of a jetliner. It is only when this energy reaches the coast that it transforms into one of nature’s most destructive forces. In this lesson, we will act as scientists to understand the “why” behind this transformation. How is this energy created, how does it travel, and what makes it so devastating?

Phenomenon: The 2004 Indian Ocean Catastrophe

On December 26, 2004, a massive submarine earthquake (Moment Magnitude 9.1) ruptured the seafloor off the coast of Sumatra, Indonesia. This event triggered the deadliest tsunami in recorded history, claiming nearly 250,000 lives across 14 countries, some many thousands of kilometers away.

The event posed a terrible scientific puzzle: How could a single earthquake in Indonesia cause catastrophic flooding hours later in places as far away as Sri Lanka, India, and even the coast of Africa?

Investigation: Modeling a Tsunami's Path

To protect a coastal community, the first thing you need to know is when the threat will arrive. A tsunami warning is a race against time.

Scientists at warning centers do not guess; they use mathematical models. They know the earthquake’s location (the epicenter) and they know the physics of wave speed. By dividing the ocean into a grid and using the depth of each grid cell, they can calculate the wave’s speed and predict its path across the entire ocean basin. The Rules of the Game: Tsunami Generation and Wave Physics

Your lab revealed a key pattern: tsunamis can cross oceans in mere hours. Now, let’s look at the formal science behind this.

1. Generation: The Vertical Shove

Most tsunamis are caused by a specific type of earthquake at a subduction zone. This is where one tectonic plate slides under another. If the plates become “stuck,” stress builds up. When the overriding plate suddenly slips and snaps upward, it shoves the entire column of water above it—all the way from the seafloor to the surface. This vertical displacement of a massive volume of water is the “birth” of the tsunami.

2. Propagation: Fast, Long, and Low

A tsunami is not like a normal wind wave. It is a “shallow-water wave,” even in the deep ocean. This is because its wavelength (the distance from one crest to the next) is enormous, often hundreds of kilometers long. The wave’s energy extends through the entire water column.

Because of this, its speed is not controlled by wind, but by water depth. The formula is $Speed \approx \sqrt{g \cdot d}$:

  • $g$ is the acceleration due to gravity ($9.8 \, m/s^2$)
  • $d$ is the water depth in meters

In the deep ocean (e.g., $d = 4000 \, m$), the speed is $\sqrt{9.8 \cdot 4000} \approx 198 \, m/s$, or over 700 km/hr! In this deep water, the wave’s energy is spread thin, so its height (amplitude) is low.

3. Shoaling: Slow, Short, and Tall

As the wave approaches the coast, the water depth ($d$) decreases.

  • According to the formula, its speed must decrease.
  • As the front of the wave slows, the back of the wave catches up, causing its wavelength to shorten.
  • The wave’s total energy has nowhere to go. It can no longer be contained in the shrinking water column, so it is forced upward, causing the wave’s height (amplitude) to grow dramatically.

This process, called shoaling, is what transforms a 1-meter-high surge in the deep ocean into a 10, 20, or even 30-meter-high wall of water at the coast.

Example 1: Calculating Tsunami Speed and Travel Time

Example 1: Calculating Tsunami Speed and Travel Time

An earthquake occurs at the Aleutian Trench, where the ocean depth is 7,000 m. A coastal community in Hawaii is 3,500 km away, and its shoreline has an average depth of 10 m.

Part A: How fast does the tsunami travel in the deep trench?

Solution:

  • Use the speed formula: $Speed = \sqrt{g \cdot d}$
  • $Speed = \sqrt{9.8 \, m/s^2 \cdot 7000 \, m}$
  • $Speed \approx 262 \, m/s$ (or about 943 km/hr)

Part B: How fast does it travel when it reaches the 10-meter-deep shoreline?

Solution:

  • $Speed = \sqrt{9.8 \, m/s^2 \cdot 10 \, m}$
  • $Speed \approx 9.9 \, m/s$ (or about 36 km/hr, a speed a person could almost outrun)

Part C: Approximately how long does the community in Hawaii have to evacuate?

Solution:

  • Use the travel time formula: $Time = Distance / Speed$
  • We must use the average speed across the basin. For this, we’ll use the deep-water speed as a rough estimate.
  • First, convert distance to meters: $3,500 \, km \cdot 1000 \, m/km = 3,500,000 \, m$
  • $Time = 3,500,000 \, m / 262 \, m/s \approx 13,359 \, s$
  • $Time \approx 13,359 \, s / 3600 \, s/hr \approx 3.7 \, hours$

This calculation is the basis of all tsunami warnings.

Common Mistake: Wind Waves vs. Tsunamis

Do not confuse tsunamis with regular waves.

  • Wind Waves are a surface event. Their energy comes from wind, and they have short wavelengths (e.g., 100 meters).
  • Tsunamis are a full-depth event. Their energy comes from geological displacement (like an earthquake), and they have massive wavelengths (e.g., 100 kilometers).

Application: Forecasting Destruction

The science of wave dynamics is not just academic. It is the tool scientists use to save lives. By understanding the physics of shoaling, we can predict which coastal areas will be hit hardest. A gently sloping bay might see a terrifyingly high wave, while a steep underwater cliff might reflect some of the energy.

Thinking Lens: Systems and System Models

The 2004 Tsunami is a powerful example of a System. An earthquake (a geological system) transferred energy to the ocean (a hydrological system), which then devastated coastal communities (human systems). No single part can be understood in isolation.

Our models—the speed formula, the travel-time maps—are our way of understanding this complex system. They are System Models. They simplify reality to make it predictable. The more accurate our model, the more lives we can save. As a scientist, your job is to build and refine these models.
