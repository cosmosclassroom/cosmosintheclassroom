Quiz 1.1: Decoding Seismic Signals

Total Points: 30
Role Focus: The Scientist
Duration: 22 minutes

Segment 1 of 3: Seismic Wave Analysis (7 minutes) - 10 points

Context: On August 23, 2011, a magnitude 5.8 earthquake centered in Virginia was felt throughout the Northeast, including in Branford, Connecticut.

Question 1

The seismograph recorded a P-wave arrival at 2:51:32 PM and an S-wave arrival at 2:51:42 PM. Calculate the S-P interval in seconds.

SOLUTION: S-P interval = S-wave arrival time - P-wave arrival time S-P interval = 2:51:42 PM - 2:51:32 PM S-P interval = 10 seconds

CORRECT ANSWER: 10 seconds

Question 2

Using your S-P interval from Question 1 and the standard travel-time graph provided by your teacher, determine the distance from Hartford to the earthquake epicenter.

SOLUTION: Using the standard relationship: Distance ≈ S-P interval × 8 km/s Distance = 10 seconds × 8 km/s = 80 km

CORRECT ANSWER: 80 kilometers

Question 3

If the P-wave traveled at an average speed of 6.0 km/s, calculate how long it took the P-wave to travel from the Virginia epicenter to Hartford. Use the distance you found in Question 2.

SOLUTION: Using: Time = Distance ÷ Speed Time = 80 km ÷ 6.0 km/s = 13.3 seconds

CORRECT ANSWER: 13.3 seconds

Question 4

The 2011 Virginia earthquake had a magnitude of 5.8, but it was felt from Georgia to Maine. Explain why a single earthquake (cause) can have very different intensity experiences (effects) in different locations.

SAMPLE HIGH-QUALITY ANSWER: The intensity of earthquake shaking varies by location due to several factors:

  1. Distance from epicenter: Places closer to the epicenter experience stronger shaking than those farther away
  2. Local geology: Areas with soft sediments or loose soil amplify shaking, while solid bedrock reduces it
  3. Building construction: Older or poorly constructed buildings shake more than newer, earthquake-resistant structures
  4. Topography: Hills and valleys can focus or scatter seismic waves differently

Even though the earthquake had one magnitude (5.8), these local factors caused different intensity experiences across the region.

Question 5

As a scientist, what would be your biggest concern about the seismic monitoring network in the Northeast based on this 2011 event?

SAMPLE HIGH-QUALITY ANSWER: The biggest concern would be the sparse seismograph network in the Northeast compared to seismically active regions like California. With fewer monitoring stations:

  • We have less precise data for locating earthquakes
  • We might miss smaller earthquakes that could provide warning signs
  • We have limited ability to provide real-time alerts to the public
  • We can’t study local geological effects as thoroughly

This 2011 event showed that significant earthquakes can affect the entire Northeast, so we need better monitoring to protect these densely populated areas.

Question 6

How many earthquakes in this dataset were felt in Branford (including “slightly felt”)?

CORRECT ANSWER: 5 earthquakes (2001, 2005, 2012, 2015, 2018)

Question 7

Based on the data, what is the approximate distance threshold below which earthquakes of magnitude 2.5 or greater are likely to be felt in Branford?

SOLUTION: Looking at earthquakes magnitude 2.5+ that were felt:

  • 2001: M2.8, 12 km - Felt slightly
  • 2005: M3.2, 15 km - Felt
  • 2012: M3.4, 8 km - Felt
  • 2015: M2.9, 14 km - Felt slightly
  • 2018: M3.1, 11 km - Felt

Not felt:

  • 2009: M2.5, 22 km - Not felt
  • 2021: M2.7, 19 km - Not felt

CORRECT ANSWER: Approximately 15-16 kilometers

Question 8

Which factor seems more important for whether an earthquake is felt in Branford: the magnitude or the distance? Explain your reasoning using specific examples from the data.

SAMPLE HIGH-QUALITY ANSWER: Distance appears to be more important than magnitude for whether an earthquake is felt in Branford.

Evidence supporting distance as primary factor:

  • The 2012 earthquake (M3.4 at 8 km) and 2018 earthquake (M3.1 at 11 km) were both felt, while stronger earthquakes farther away were not
  • The 2021 earthquake had a moderate magnitude (M2.7) but was not felt because it was 19 km away
  • All earthquakes felt in Branford were within 15 km, regardless of magnitude
  • The 2009 earthquake (M2.5 at 22 km) was not felt despite being above the magnitude threshold

Magnitude still matters: Higher magnitude earthquakes are felt at greater distances (the M3.2 earthquake was felt at 15 km), but distance appears to be the limiting factor.

Question 9

Describe the pattern you observe between earthquake magnitude, distance from Branford, and whether the earthquake was felt. How does this pattern help scientists predict which future earthquakes will affect our community?

SAMPLE HIGH-QUALITY ANSWER: Pattern observed:

  1. Distance threshold: Earthquakes closer than ~15 km are likely to be felt, those farther than ~18 km are typically not felt
  2. Magnitude effect: Higher magnitude earthquakes can be felt at slightly greater distances
  3. Combined effect: Both factors matter, but distance appears to be the primary limiting factor

Prediction implications: This pattern helps scientists create a “felt earthquake zone” around Branford. They can predict that:

  • Any earthquake M2.5+ within 15 km will likely be felt
  • Earthquakes M3.0+ might be felt up to ~17 km away
  • Earthquakes beyond 20 km are unlikely to be felt regardless of magnitude
  • This allows for better public communication about which earthquakes to expect residents will notice

Question 10

Based on your analysis, what key information would you provide to engineers designing earthquake-resistant structures in Branford?

SAMPLE HIGH-QUALITY ANSWER: Based on the data analysis, I would provide engineers with the following key information:

Expected earthquake parameters for Branford:

  • Magnitude range: Plan for earthquakes up to M3.5 based on local historical data
  • Frequency: Small earthquakes (M2.5-3.5) occur regularly in the region
  • Local amplification: Ground shaking may be amplified due to our proximity (8-15 km) to the active Moodus seismic zone

Design recommendations:

  • Structures should be designed to handle moderate shaking from nearby small-to-moderate earthquakes
  • Consider cumulative effects of frequent small earthquakes on building fatigue
  • While major earthquakes are rare, the 2011 Virginia event shows that larger, distant earthquakes can still affect the area

Confidence level: High confidence for M2.5-3.5 events, moderate confidence for larger events based on limited historical data.

Segment 3 of 3: Scientific Limitations (7 minutes) - 10 points

Context: In 2020, seismologists predicted a 30% chance of a magnitude 6.0+ earthquake striking the East Coast within 50 years. However, earthquake prediction remains challenging.

Question 11

For each limitation below, explain how it could affect the accuracy of risk assessments:

a) Incomplete historical records (Only ~400 years of written records in New England):

SAMPLE ANSWER: With only 400 years of records, we may be missing information about large, infrequent earthquakes that occur every 500-1000 years. This could lead to underestimating the risk of major earthquakes, causing inadequate preparation for rare but devastating events.

b) Sparse seismograph network (Fewer monitoring stations than in California):

SAMPLE ANSWER: Fewer seismographs mean less precise earthquake location and magnitude determination. This could lead to inaccurate hazard maps and poor understanding of local geological effects, potentially under- or over-estimating risk in specific areas.

c) Infrequent large events (Long return periods between major earthquakes):

SAMPLE ANSWER: With centuries between major earthquakes, we have very few data points to analyze patterns or verify our risk models. This makes it difficult to accurately predict the probability and timing of future large earthquakes.

Question 12

A proposed budget cut would reduce funding for the Northeast seismic monitoring network by 40%. Calculate the impact:

Current network: 25 seismograph stations

a) Stations remaining after budget cut: 25 stations × (100% - 40%) = 25 × 0.60 = 15 stations

b) Percentage of coverage lost (%): 40% (this is given in the problem)

Question 13

Choose one data limitation from Problem 11. Explain how this scientific limitation (cause) could lead to poor engineering designs or policy decisions (effect) that put communities at risk.

SAMPLE HIGH-QUALITY ANSWER (choosing “incomplete historical records”):

Scientific limitation (cause): Incomplete historical records mean we don’t know about major earthquakes that occurred before 1600 in New England.

Poor decisions (effects):

  1. Engineering: Buildings might be designed for smaller earthquakes than actually possible, leading to structural failures during a rare but large earthquake
  2. Policy: Communities might adopt less stringent building codes, thinking the earthquake risk is lower than it really is
  3. Emergency planning: Cities might not prepare adequate emergency response plans for major earthquakes, leading to poor disaster response

Risk to communities: When the rare but large earthquake eventually occurs, buildings collapse, people are unprepared, and the community suffers much more damage and loss of life than necessary.

Question 14

How has thinking like a scientist in this quiz changed your understanding of earthquake risk in Connecticut? What questions would you now ask when you hear news reports about earthquake predictions?

SAMPLE HIGH-QUALITY ANSWER: Changed understanding: I now realize that earthquake risk in Connecticut is more complex than I thought. Even though we’re not on a major fault line, we do have real seismic risk from both local earthquakes and distant large ones. The data limitations mean there’s more uncertainty than I expected.

Questions for news reports:

  • What data is this prediction based on? How many years of historical records?
  • How many seismograph stations collected this data?
  • What’s the uncertainty range in this prediction?
  • Are they accounting for rare, large earthquakes that might not be in the historical record?

ASSESSMENT SCORING RUBRIC

Data Analysis & Interpretation (/10)

Exceeding (10 pts) Can apply advanced statistical or computational analysis to reveal subtle patterns, trends, and correlations in complex data sets, and rigorously evaluate the statistical significance and limitations of the findings.

Meeting (9 pts) Can analyze data using tools, technologies, and/or models to make valid and reliable scientific claims. Can consider the limitations of data analysis (e.g., measurement error) and compare various data sets to examine consistency.

Approaching (7.5 pts) Can construct, analyze, and interpret graphical displays of data to identify linear and nonlinear relationships. Can use graphical displays to analyze large data sets and distinguish between causal and correlational relationships.

Developing (5.5 pts) Can represent data in tables and graphical displays, analyze and interpret data to make sense of phenomena, and compare data collected by different groups.

Mathematical Modeling (/10)

Exceeding (10 pts) Can develop and apply original computational models or mathematical algorithms to simulate complex phenomena, solve problems that are not tractable with standard methods, and generate novel predictions.

Meeting (9 pts) Can use mathematical representations of phenomena to describe and support claims. Can use a computational model to illustrate the relationships between systems or their components.

Approaching (7.5 pts) Can use mathematical representations to describe and support scientific conclusions and design solutions. Can apply mathematical concepts and processes to scientific questions.

Developing (5.5 pts) Can decide if qualitative or quantitative data are needed for an investigation and can use digital tools to organize and analyze data to see patterns.

Explanation & Design (/10)

Exceeding (10 pts) Can construct comprehensive, evidence-based explanations for novel or complex phenomena that synthesize principles from multiple disciplines, and design innovative solutions that optimize across multiple, competing constraints.

Meeting (9 pts) Can make a quantitative and/or qualitative claim about the relationship between variables. Can construct and revise an explanation based on valid and reliable evidence. Can design, evaluate, and refine a solution to a complex real-world problem.

Approaching (7.5 pts) Can construct an explanation that includes qualitative or quantitative relationships between variables. Can apply scientific principles to design an object, tool, process or system.

Developing (5.5 pts) Can construct an explanation of observed relationships and use evidence to support that explanation. Can apply scientific ideas to solve simple design problems.

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS TO ADDRESS:

  1. Time calculation errors: Students often struggle with time differences across minutes/hours
  2. Distance/speed confusion: Mixing up the relationship between distance, speed, and time
  3. Data interpretation: Not recognizing patterns in the earthquake data table
  4. Cause-and-effect reasoning: Difficulty connecting scientific limitations to real-world consequences

DIFFERENTIATION NOTES:

For Advanced Students:

  • Ask them to calculate the energy difference between the largest and smallest earthquakes in the data set
  • Have them research the geological reasons for the Moodus seismic zone

For Struggling Students:

  • Provide additional practice with time calculations
  • Use graphic organizers for the data analysis questions
  • Allow use of calculators for all mathematical problems

For ELL Students:

  • Provide vocabulary support for technical terms
  • Allow extra time for reading and writing responses
  • Focus on mathematical problems which are less language-dependent
