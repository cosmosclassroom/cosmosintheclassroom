Hexagon Lab 1.3: Forensic Engineering Analysis

Objective: Use simulated test data to analyze various structural designs, identify critical points of failure, and recommend the most resilient, cost-effective building strategy for earthquake-prone areas.

Introduction: From Failure to Forensic Science

In the world of structural engineering, failure is not an end—it is the ultimate teacher. Our initial plan was to build and test our own structures, but we encountered a real-world engineering problem: material failure due to environmental conditions. The “joints” of our test models (the marshmallows) failed before testing could be completed.

However, this provides a unique opportunity. Before the material failure, an initial set of five prototype towers were tested on a shake table. The data from those tests was saved.

Your mission is to act as a forensic engineer. You will not build a physical model. Instead, you will analyze the performance and cost data from these five prototypes to understand why they failed and to determine the principles of a successful design. Your analysis will form the core of your Engineer’s Proposal.

The Dataset: Prototype Performance on the Shake Table

The following five structures were built using the same materials. They were each subjected to increasingly intense P-waves (vertical shaking) and S-waves (horizontal shaking), with intensity rated on a scale of 1 to 5. The test was stopped once the structure experienced a critical failure.

Investigation: Your Engineering Analysis

In your Research Log, create a new entry for Hexagon Lab 1.3. Work through the following analytical steps, recording your findings for each.

1. Cost Calculation

Calculate the total cost for each of the five prototypes and fill in the “Total Cost” column in your notes.

2. Performance vs. Force Type

  • Which design principle appears to be most effective at resisting P-waves (vertical shaking)?
  • Which design principle appears to be most effective at resisting S-waves (horizontal shaking)?

3. Point of Failure Analysis

  • Look at Prototypes Alpha, Delta, and Echo. What was the most common component to fail: the structural members (toothpicks) or the joints (marshmallows)?
  • Prototype Charlie used cross-bracing and its joints held strong, but the members failed. What does this tell you about the importance of balancing the strength of different components in a system?

4. Cost-Benefit Analysis

  • Prototype Bravo was the strongest, but also the most expensive. Calculate its “cost per level of S-wave resistance” (Total Cost ÷ Max S-Wave Survived).
  • Prototype Charlie was the second strongest. Calculate its cost per level of S-wave resistance.
  • Which of these two designs provides the most “resilience for the dollar”? Explain your reasoning.

Synthesis: From Data to Recommendation

Now, use your analysis to answer the final questions in your Research Log. These answers will be directly used in the Engineer’s Proposal section of your Unit 1 Research Brief.

Design Recommendation

Based on the evidence from all five tests, what single design feature (e.g., wider base, cross-bracing, reinforced joints) provides the biggest improvement in S-wave resistance for a reasonable cost?

Policy Proposal

Imagine you are advising your local town council. Based only on the evidence from this lab, what is one building code policy you would recommend? (e.g., “All new construction must include foundational cross-bracing.”) Justify your policy using specific data from the tests.

Structure and Function

How does this lab demonstrate the cross-cutting concept of “Structure and Function”? Explain how a change in a tower’s structure (e.g., adding bracing) directly affects its ability to perform its function (resisting seismic forces).
