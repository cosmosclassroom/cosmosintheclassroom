Research Brief: Flooding & Landslides

SPHYS • Research brief • Unit 4
Phase 1 of 3

Phase 1: Forecast

After encountering the anchoring phenomenon—a local, major flooding event—your first task is to document your initial observations, questions, and ideas about how land and water interact.

Observe the anchoring phenomenon: A recent major flooding event in Connecticut, which also led to landslides in some areas. In the spaces below, list at least three things you notice about this type of event and three things you now wonder about why and where they happen.

Based on what you already know, describe your first model of a community designed to be resilient to heavy rain. What key features would you include to manage water and keep hillsides stable? List the features and briefly explain their purpose.

How does this phenomenon of flooding and landslides relate to what you know about geography, weather, or even just driving or walking around your own town?

Phase 2: Blueprint

You have now used maps to identify vulnerable areas and analyzed potential solutions. It is time to synthesize your scientific and engineering findings to define the interconnected problems.

Based on ‘Hexagon Lab 4.1: Mapping Vulnerability,’ explain how you can use a topographical map to identify a floodplain. What specific characteristics of the map tell you that an area is at high risk for flooding?

Floods and landslides are often linked. Explain the concept of ‘soil saturation’ in your own words. How can an extreme rainfall event trigger a landslide on an unstable slope? What map features indicate a high risk for landslides?

Based on ‘Hexagon Lab 4.2: Evaluating Mitigation Strategies,’ propose one specific engineering solution to mitigate flood risk in a vulnerable area (e.g., levee, retention pond). Using data from the lab, discuss the trade-offs of your choice in terms of cost vs. benefit.

Now, propose one specific engineering solution to stabilize an at-risk hillside (e.g., retaining wall, terracing, revegetation). What are the primary trade-offs for this type of project in terms of cost, effectiveness, and environmental impact?

Phase 3: Reflection

Your investigation is complete. The final step is to construct your definitive, evidence-based recommendation on how to best invest in your community’s safety.

This is your final recommendation to the town’s planning and zoning commission. You have a limited budget and can only fund ONE major project next year. Your response must be a multi-paragraph brief that explicitly addresses the following:

  1. The Situation: Summarize the interconnected risks for a non-expert. Explain how a single storm creates a dual threat of flooding in low-lying areas and landslides on steep slopes.
  2. The Options: Briefly present the two distinct solutions you proposed in your Blueprint—one for flood control and one for slope stabilization.
  3. The Recommendation: Choose ONE project to prioritize for funding. You must justify your choice with a cost-benefit analysis. Which hazard poses a greater immediate threat to the community? Which solution provides the most protection for the cost? Argue your position clearly using evidence.

Accompany your synthesis with a detailed written description of your final, revised model. Describe your recommended project (e.g., the levee or the retaining wall). Where would it be built? What would it look like? How would it function during an extreme storm?

Look back at your initial model of a ‘resilient community’ from the Forecast. How has your understanding of the problem’s complexity changed? What specific piece of evidence (from a map or a data table) was most responsible for clarifying your thinking?

This unit was about making tough choices with limited public funds. How has this process changed the way you might think about local news stories regarding infrastructure, taxes, and community planning?
