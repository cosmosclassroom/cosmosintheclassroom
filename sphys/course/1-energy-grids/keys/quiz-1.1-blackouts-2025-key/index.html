1. Power Calculation (4 points)

A family uses a small gasoline generator that provides 120 V to power a 60 W electric heater.

Given: V = 120 V, P = 60 W
Find: The current I drawn by the heater.

SOLUTION:

Starting with the power equation: P = VI

Solving for current: I = P/V

Substituting values: I = 60 W ÷ 120 V = 0.50 A

CORRECT ANSWER: 0.50 A (amperes)

GRADING RUBRIC:

  • 4 points: Correct formula, correct substitution, correct answer with proper unit
  • 3 points: Correct formula and substitution, minor calculation error or missing unit
  • 2 points: Correct formula identified, major calculation error
  • 1 point: Some understanding of relationship between P, V, and I shown
  • 0 points: No correct work shown

COMMON ERRORS:

  • Using V = IR instead of P = VI (formula confusion)
  • Calculating I = V/P instead of I = P/V (algebra error)
  • Forgetting units or using wrong units

2. Energy Cost Calculation (6 points)

A different family powers a 1,500 W space heater for 4 hours using a generator. The cost of gasoline to run the generator is about $0.50 per kilowatt-hour (kWh).

Given: P = 1500 W, Δt = 4 hours, Cost = $0.50/kWh
Find: The total cost to run the heater.

SOLUTION:

Step 1: Calculate energy consumed E = P × Δt = 1500 W × 4 h = 6000 Wh

Step 2: Convert to kilowatt-hours E = 6000 Wh ÷ 1000 = 6.0 kWh

Step 3: Calculate total cost Cost = Energy × Rate = 6.0 kWh × $0.50/kWh = $3.00

CORRECT ANSWER: 3.00 (dollars)

GRADING RUBRIC:

  • 6 points: All steps correct, proper unit conversions, correct final answer
  • 5 points: Correct process, minor calculation error or unit issue
  • 4 points: Correct energy calculation, error in cost calculation
  • 3 points: Shows understanding of E = P×t, significant errors in conversion or cost
  • 2 points: Attempts correct approach, multiple errors
  • 1 point: Some relevant formula used
  • 0 points: No correct work shown

COMMON ERRORS:

  • Forgetting to convert watts to kilowatts
  • Using wrong time units
  • Calculation errors in multiplication/division
  • Not showing conversion steps

4. Connecting Models to Real Systems (7 points)

SAMPLE HIGH-QUALITY ANSWER:

In our lab, we observed that appliances in a house are connected in parallel circuits, not series. When we unplugged one appliance (like turning off one bulb), the others continued to work normally because each device has its own separate path to the power source.

The Texas power grid works similarly to this parallel circuit model. When grid operators implemented rolling blackouts, they were essentially “unplugging” certain neighborhoods (like removing one parallel branch) while keeping other parts of the grid energized. Because neighborhoods are connected in parallel rather than series, cutting power to one area doesn’t cause the whole system to shut down.

This prevented total grid collapse because it reduced the overall electrical demand (load) on the remaining power generation plants. Just like removing one bulb from a parallel circuit reduces the total current draw, removing neighborhoods from the grid reduced the total power demand to a level that the struggling power plants could still supply.

Without this parallel design and rolling blackouts, the entire grid would have experienced a cascading failure when demand exceeded supply.

GRADING RUBRIC (7 points):

  • 7 points: Clear parallel circuit connection, explains isolation capability, connects to demand management, uses proper terminology
  • 5-6 points: Good parallel circuit explanation, adequate connection to grid behavior, minor gaps
  • 3-4 points: Basic understanding of parallel circuits, limited connection to grid system
  • 1-2 points: Some circuit knowledge shown, poor connection to real system
  • 0 points: No clear understanding demonstrated

KEY CONCEPTS STUDENTS MUST ADDRESS:

  • Parallel circuit behavior (separate paths)
  • System isolation capability
  • Prevention of total failure
  • Load/demand management

5b. Explain the Connection (8 points)

SAMPLE HIGH-QUALITY ANSWER:

When we connected two bulbs in series in our lab, they both dimmed because they had to share the available electrical energy, and the total resistance increased, reducing current flow.

This connects to the Texas power crisis through the concept of supply and demand. During the winter storm, millions of Texans simultaneously turned on electric heaters, creating a massive spike in electrical demand (like adding more bulbs to the circuit). However, at the same time, many power generation plants were failing due to frozen equipment, reducing the available supply of electricity.

Just like how our series bulbs dimmed when they had to share limited power, the Texas grid became stressed when demand exceeded the system’s capacity to generate and deliver electricity. When electrical demand is much higher than supply, the system becomes unstable and can experience voltage drops, equipment overloads, and ultimately cascading failures.

The grid has physical limits on how much electricity it can generate and transmit, just like our simple circuit had limits. When those limits are exceeded, the system cannot maintain stable operation. Rolling blackouts were implemented to artificially reduce demand (like removing bulbs from the circuit) to bring the system back within its operational limits and prevent a complete statewide blackout.

This demonstrates how individual actions (everyone turning on heaters) can stress shared infrastructure systems beyond their designed capacity, leading to system-wide failures that affect everyone.

GRADING RUBRIC (8 points):

  • 8 points: Clear connection between lab observation and grid crisis, explains demand spike, discusses system limits, addresses cascading effects
  • 6-7 points: Good connection made, addresses most key concepts with minor gaps
  • 4-5 points: Basic connection shown, understands demand concept, limited depth
  • 2-3 points: Some understanding of energy demand, weak connection to lab
  • 1 point: Minimal understanding shown
  • 0 points: No clear connection or understanding demonstrated

KEY CONCEPTS FOR FULL CREDIT:

  • Connection between series dimming and shared/limited resources
  • Demand spike explanation (millions using heaters simultaneously)
  • Limited system capacity concept
  • Cascading failure prevention through rolling blackouts
  • Systems thinking about individual actions affecting shared infrastructure
