Overview

Abstract

In this investigation, you will act as an engineer on a community planning board. Your task is to use a quantitative engineering tool—the decision matrix—to evaluate three different energy sources. By assigning weights to competing criteria such as cost, reliability, and environmental impact, you will make a data-driven recommendation and justify the inevitable trade-offs your choice requires.

Introduction

As we learned from the Texas power crisis, there is no single “perfect” energy source. Building a reliable grid is a constant balancing act. A solution that is cheap might not be reliable. A solution that is environmentally friendly might require a lot of land. Engineers must weigh these competing factors to make the best possible decision for their community. Today, you will use the same process to analyze the trade-offs for yourself.

You have been provided with a data table containing key statistics for three potential power plant options for a new community. Your task is to use this data to perform a formal trade-off analysis.

Synthesis: Claim-Evidence-Reasoning

Connection to Unit Phenomenon

This lab simulates the exact decisions that grid planners in Texas face. The pre-crisis Texas grid was heavily optimized for one criterion: low cost, which led to a heavy reliance on natural gas. However, this came with trade-offs, including vulnerability to extreme cold and significant CO2 emissions. Your work in this lab—weighing competing priorities to design a more resilient system—is the central challenge of modern engineering. The ‘right’ answer depends entirely on the values and priorities of the community you serve.
