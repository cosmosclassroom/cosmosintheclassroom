Hexagon Lab 1.4: Engineering Design Tradeoffs

Today’s Question: How do engineers use data to make difficult decisions when there is no single “best” choice?

Complete this lab in your Science Notebook.

What You Need:

  • Your Science Notebook
  • Calculator
  • Access to the Energy Source Data Webpage

Background:

After the 2021 power crisis, many Texas communities realized they needed more reliable energy. Imagine your town must choose a new power plant. As engineers, your team must analyze the data and recommend a solution. This process always involves balancing competing priorities, or trade-offs.

The Data:

Your team has been given data comparing three potential energy sources.

Energy Source Cost per kWh (USD) Land Use (m² per GWh) Reliability (%) CO₂ Emissions (tons per GWh)
Solar Farm $0.08 15,000 85% 50
Wind Farm $0.06 25,000 78% 25
Natural Gas Plant $0.07 2,000 95% 800

Part 1: Look for Patterns

Step 1: In your notebook, answer these questions based on the data table.

  1. Which source has the lowest cost?
  2. Which source requires the least land?
  3. Which source is the most reliable?
  4. Which source produces the least CO₂?

Make a Claim: In your notebook, complete this sentence: “Based on our initial look at the data, there is no single ‘best’ energy source because…”

Your Evidence: In your notebook, give one example of a trade-off from the data. For instance, “The source with the lowest cost also has…”

Part 2: Do the Math

Engineers use a tool called a Decision Matrix to make choices based on what’s most important. You will use this tool to make a recommendation for your assigned community.

Step 1: Get Your Community Assignment Your teacher will assign your group one of the following communities. Copy the Community Type and its Weights into your notebook. The weight shows how important each criterion is (5 = most important).

  • Agricultural Community: Values low costs and protecting farmland.
    • Cost (Weight: 5), Land Use (Weight: 5), Reliability (Weight: 3), Environment (Weight: 2)
  • Environmental Community: Values fighting climate change.
    • Cost (Weight: 2), Land Use (Weight: 3), Reliability (Weight: 3), Environment (Weight: 5)
  • Industrial Community: Values steady, reliable power for factories.
    • Cost (Weight: 4), Land Use (Weight: 2), Reliability (Weight: 5), Environment (Weight: 2)

Step 2: Score Each Source Create a new table in your notebook. For each criterion, score the energy sources from 1 to 5, where 5 is the best. (For Cost, Land Use, and Emissions, the lowest number gets the highest score).

Criteria Solar Score Wind Score Gas Score
Cost per kWh 3 5 4
Land Use 3 2 5
Reliability 3 2 5
Environment (CO₂) 4 5 1

Step 3: Calculate the Weighted Score Now, create your final Decision Matrix in your notebook. For each energy source, multiply its Score by your community’s Weight for that criterion.

Part 3: Connect to the Big Picture

Think About It: Your task is to write a scientific argument to convince the town council. Use the Claim-Evidence-Reasoning (CER) framework to structure your recommendation.

Your Prompt: “Which energy source should your community choose for their new power plant?”

Write your response in your notebook using these sentence starters:

CLAIM (What is your recommendation?):

“Based on our analysis, the [your community type] should choose the [winning energy source] power plant.”

EVIDENCE (What data supports your claim?):

“Our decision matrix shows that [winning energy source] had the highest total score of [total score]. This was because it performed well on [criterion 1] and [criterion 2], which our community weighted heavily at [weight 1] and [weight 2].”

REASONING (Why does the evidence support your claim?):

“This decision connects directly to our community’s priorities. Because our community values [community value], choosing the option that maximizes [relevant criterion] is the logical choice. While this means accepting a trade-off in [weaker criterion], it is the best overall fit for what our community needs to succeed.”

Share: Compare your final recommendation with a team that had a different community assignment. Why did you get different answers even though you used the same data?
