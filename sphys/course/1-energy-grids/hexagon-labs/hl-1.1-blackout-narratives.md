Part 1: Data and Narrative Analysis
Instructions: Read the following three passages carefully. Each provides a different perspective on the crisis.
Passage A: The Human Cost
The devastating storm in Texas this February killed at least 111 people, a number nearly double the earlier estimates. The crisis claimed victims from 47 counties across the state. Power grid failures caused millions to wait in the dark, in homes with no heat or proper insulation. An 11-year-old boy was found dead from hypothermia after his family's mobile home lost power. Countless others died from accidents on unsafe roads, falls, and fires. People who relied on electricity for medical equipment died when they couldn't maintain power. Still others were poisoned by carbon monoxide while attempting to stay warm by heating their homes with a stove or running their cars in garages.
In Dallas, 51-year-old Stephanie Murdoch hid in her condo, wearing two pairs of pants, two sweaters, three pairs of socks, a hat, gloves, and as many blankets as she could find. She grew angrier at the power companies, who clearly had not prepared. "We've got another blast of snow coming in...and still no clear answers as to why the grids aren't working better," Murdoch said. For children, the storm was another blow on top of the COVID-19 pandemic. Rosa Mendoza, a mother in East Dallas, worried about the anxiety the storm caused her seven-year-old son. "He has dealt with so much," she said, reflecting on the cancellation of his school's "100th Day" celebration, a small joy lost in a year of disruption.
Passage B: The Blame on Wind
Nearly half of the wind-power capacity of Texas was knocked out by ice storms. Millions of people lost power, and dozens died. Texans want to know: who was to blame? The Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT), the organization that controls the electricity grid, said that some windmills seized up from the cold.
As an Arctic air mass pushed southward, ERCOT issued an alert pleading with consumers and businesses to use less electricity because the energy demand was so high. "We are dealing with higher-than-normal generation outages due to frozen wind turbines and limited natural gas supplies available to generating units," the agency said. Wind farms in West Texas were particularly hard-hit. According to ERCOT, wind generation accounted for 23% of Texas electricity generation in 2021. This is the second-largest energy source behind natural gas, which represented 45%.
Passage C: A Different Story
Some people say an overreliance on wind-power caused the problem. "We should never build another wind turbine in Texas. The experiment failed big-time," Texas Agriculture Commissioner Sid Miller wrote in a Facebook post that was shared 9,000 times. But are windmills really the problem? Wind, solar, and other renewables only make up a small part of the power infrastructure in Texas. The state relies mostly on natural gas for electricity.
ERCOT's own data from January shows that only around 25% of Texas electricity last year came from wind, while 60% came from natural gas and coal. According to The Texas Tribune, ERCOT officials said natural gas providers were mostly to be blamed for the outages, not renewable sources. In fact, windmills actually performed relatively well compared to other energy sources during the crisis. Mark Henry, a county judge, was frustrated with the lack of clear information. "If they had been honest with us from the beginning, we would have ordered evacuations," he said. "But they didn't tell us that."
