▶ Key Vocabulary (8 terms)
Plate Tectonics

The scientific theory that Earth’s outer shell (lithosphere) is divided into several rigid plates that glide over the semi-molten mantle (asthenosphere).

Plate Boundary

The region where two tectonic plates meet.

Convergent Boundary

A boundary where two plates collide. Can result in subduction (one plate goes under) or mountain building.

Divergent Boundary

A boundary where two plates move apart. Characterized by rift valleys on land and mid-ocean ridges in the sea.

Transform Boundary

A boundary where two plates slide horizontally past each other. Characterized by earthquakes.

Radiometric Dating

A technique used to date materials such as rocks by comparing the observed abundance of a naturally occurring radioactive isotope and its decay products.

Slab Pull

A major driving force for plate motion; the gravitational pull of a dense, subducting plate sinking into the mantle.

Ridge Push

A driving force for plate motion; the gravitational force acting on the elevated mid-ocean ridge, causing the plate to slide ‘downhill’.

Introduction: A Dynamic Surface

In Chapter 3, we uncovered Earth’s internal engine: mantle convection fueled by radioactive decay. Now, we turn our attention to the surface, where the effects of this engine are dramatically visible. This chapter explores the unifying theory of Plate Tectonics – the grand narrative explaining how continents drift, oceans open and close, mountains rise, and earthquakes shake our world. We’ll examine the interactions at plate boundaries and the specific forces that orchestrate this planetary dance.

Phenomenon: A Rip in the Crust (Revisited)

The massive rift in Ethiopia’s Afar Triangle, our anchoring phenomenon, is a textbook example of plate tectonics in action. It represents a divergent plate boundary on land – a place where the African continent is literally being torn apart, potentially forming a new ocean basin over millions of years. Understanding the forces and processes at work here is key to understanding how all of Earth’s surface features are formed and evolve.

Investigation: Measuring a Moving Planet with GPS

How do we know the plates are actually moving? While geological evidence like magnetic striping on the seafloor provided early proof, modern technology gives us direct measurements. The Global Positioning System (GPS), used for everyday navigation, can pinpoint locations on Earth’s surface with millimeter accuracy. By tracking the positions of permanent scientific GPS stations bolted to bedrock on different continents over years, scientists can directly measure the slow, steady drift of the tectonic plates.

The Rules of the Game: Plate Boundaries and Driving Forces

The theory of Plate Tectonics provides a framework for understanding nearly all of Earth’s major geological features. These features are predominantly formed at the boundaries where plates interact.

Types of Plate Boundaries

There are three main ways plates interact:

  1. Divergent Boundaries: Plates move apart. Magma rises from the mantle to fill the gap, creating new crust. On continents, this forms rift valleys (like the Afar Triangle). In oceans, it forms mid-ocean ridges, the longest mountain ranges on Earth.

  2. Convergent Boundaries: Plates move together. The outcome depends on the types of crust involved:
    • Oceanic-Continental: The denser oceanic plate subducts (dives beneath) the continental plate, forming a deep ocean trench and volcanic mountain ranges on the continent (e.g., the Andes).
    • Oceanic-Oceanic: One oceanic plate subducts beneath another, forming a trench and a chain of volcanic islands (e.g., Japan, Aleutian Islands).
    • Continental-Continental: Neither plate subducts easily, resulting in intense compression, folding, and the formation of massive mountain ranges (e.g., the Himalayas).
  3. Transform Boundaries: Plates slide horizontally past each other. Crust is neither created nor destroyed, but significant friction builds up, leading to large earthquakes (e.g., the San Andreas Fault in California).

The locations of earthquakes and volcanoes worldwide map out these plate boundaries with remarkable precision, providing strong evidence for the theory.

Forces Driving Plate Motion

While mantle convection provides the underlying engine, several specific forces act directly on the plates:

  • Slab Pull: As the cold, dense leading edge of an oceanic plate sinks (subducts) into the hotter, less dense mantle at a convergent boundary, gravity pulls the rest of the plate along behind it. This is considered the strongest driving force.
  • Ridge Push: At divergent boundaries (mid-ocean ridges), the newly formed, hot crust is elevated. Gravity causes this elevated ridge to “push” the older, cooler plate away from the ridge crest.
  • Mantle Drag: The friction between the moving plate and the underlying mantle (asthenosphere) can either assist or resist motion, depending on the direction of convection currents relative to the plate.

The net force resulting from the combination of these forces (calculated using vector addition, as practiced in Problem Set 2.1) determines the speed and direction of a plate’s motion.

Summary of Key Concepts:

  • Plate Tectonics: Earth’s surface is made of moving plates.
  • Plate Boundaries: Where plates interact (divergent, convergent, transform), creating geological features.
  • Driving Forces: Primarily slab pull and ridge push, stemming from gravity acting on density and elevation differences, originating from mantle convection.

Application: Reading Geologic History & Radiometric Dating

Plate tectonics doesn’t just explain present-day features; it allows us to reconstruct Earth’s past. By analyzing rock types, fossil distributions, and paleomagnetism (ancient magnetic fields locked in rocks), geologists can map how continents have drifted, collided, and broken apart over hundreds of millions of years.

A key tool for building this history is Radiometric Dating. By measuring the ratio of remaining radioactive parent isotopes (like Uranium-235) to stable daughter isotopes (like Lead-207) in a rock sample, and knowing the parent’s half-life, scientists can calculate how long ago the rock solidified. This uses the same radioactive decay equation ($N(t) = N_0 (\frac{1}{2})^{\frac{t}{T_{1/2}}}$) we encountered in Chapter 3, but often involves solving for the time, $t$. This technique provides the absolute ages needed to calibrate the geologic timescale and reconstruct tectonic events like the formation and breakup of supercontinents or the creation of features like North America’s Midcontinent Rift.

Thinking Lens: Patterns at a Global Scale

The theory of Plate Tectonics is a triumph of scientific observation and synthesis. Individually, earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain ranges, and mid-ocean ridges might seem like isolated phenomena. But when viewed on a global scale, clear patterns emerge. Earthquakes and volcanoes occur in distinct belts that outline the plate boundaries. Mountain ranges mark zones of collision. Mid-ocean ridges trace lines of divergence. Recognizing these large-scale patterns, revealed by analyzing data globally (like the GPS data in Lab 2.1), was the key to unlocking the simple, underlying mechanism—moving plates—that governs our planet’s complex surface geology.
