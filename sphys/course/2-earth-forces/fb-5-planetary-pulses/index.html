5.1 A Crack in the World

For most of human history, the ground beneath our feet has been the very definition of solid, stable, and permanent. Continents drift, but with a slowness that makes a glacier seem like a sprinter. We build our lives on the confident assumption that the Earth will, for the most part, stay put.

Then, on September 14, 2005, in the dusty Afar desert of Ethiopia, that assumption was shattered.

It began with a swarm of earthquakes. Then, the ground cracked open. In a geological instant—a mere ten days—a fissure 35 miles long and up to 26 feet wide ripped through the desert floor. Scientists who rushed to the scene were stunned. They had witnessed the birth of what could one day be a new ocean, a process they thought took millions of years, accomplished in less than two weeks.

The Afar Rift is our new anchoring phenomenon. It is a profound and unsettling puzzle. It defies our everyday experience and forces us to ask a fundamental question: What could possibly do that? To even begin to answer this, we must first understand the opposite: why doesn’t this happen all the time?

5.2 A Universe That Resists: The Law of Inertia

A book resting on your desk is not moving. It will remain exactly where it is until you or something else interacts with it. A hockey puck gliding across perfectly smooth ice will continue to glide in a straight line at a constant speed until it hits a wall.

These scenarios reveal a deep property of the universe. Objects at rest tend to stay at rest, and objects in motion tend to stay in motion. This tendency to resist changes in motion is called inertia. It is a property of all matter.

In the 17th century, Isaac Newton formalized this observation into what we now know as Newton’s First Law of Motion, or the Law of Inertia. It states that an object will remain at rest or in uniform motion in a straight line unless acted upon by an external force.

A force is simply a push or a pull. It is the agent of change. To understand why the Afar Rift happened, we must be looking for a force—an unimaginably large one, capable of overcoming the inertia of an entire continent.

5.3 The Unseen Tug of War: Balanced and Unbalanced Forces

If you and a friend push on opposite sides of a box with equal force, the box doesn’t move. The forces cancel each other out. This is a state of balanced forces. The net force—the sum of all forces acting on the object—is zero.

When forces are balanced, there is no change in motion. The ground beneath your feet is, right now, in a state of (mostly) balanced forces, with the downward pull of gravity perfectly countered by the upward push of the rock below it.

But what happens if another friend joins in and pushes with you? Now the forces are unequal. They are unbalanced. The net force is no longer zero, and the box begins to move. It accelerates in the direction of the stronger push.

For the Afar Rift to have occurred, a previously balanced system of forces deep within the Earth must have suddenly become unbalanced. For millennia, the forces holding the African tectonic plate together were in equilibrium with the forces trying to pull it apart. Then, in September 2005, the pulling force must have become dramatically, catastrophically greater. The result was a rupture—a rapid, violent change.

5.4 Pause and Model: Your First Ideas

We now have the first tools of our investigation: inertia tells us that the continent should have stayed put, and the concept of force tells us that an unbalanced push or pull must be the culprit. This is the point where we form a first hypothesis.
