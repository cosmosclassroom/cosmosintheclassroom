Problem

This problem models the decay of a heat-producing isotope in the Earth's mantle over geologic time.

Potassium-40 is a major source of heat in the Earth’s mantle. It has a half-life ($T_{1/2}$) of 1.25 billion years.

If a sample of mantle rock formed with $N_0 = 80.0 \text{ g}$ of Potassium-40, how much Potassium-40 ($N(t)$) would be left after $t = 2.50 \text{ billion years}$?

Given:

  • $N_0 = 80.0 \text{ g}$
  • $T_{1/2} = 1.25 \text{ billion years}$
  • $t = 2.50 \text{ billion years}$

Find: $N(t)$

Step 1: Identify Givens and Unknowns

  • $N_0 = 80.0 \text{ g}$
  • $T_{1/2} = 1.25 \text{ billion years}$
  • $t = 2.50 \text{ billion years}$
  • Unknown: $N(t) = ?$

Step 2: Select Parent Equation

\[N(t) = N_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{t}{T_{1/2}}}\]

Step 3: Substitute Values

First, find the number of half-lives: \(\frac{t}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{2.50 \text{ billion years}}{1.25 \text{ billion years}} = 2.00 \text{ half-lives}\)

Now, substitute into the equation:

\[N(t) = 80.0 \text{ g} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2.00}\] \[N(t) = 80.0 \text{ g} \left(\frac{1}{4}\right)\] \[N(t) = 20.0 \text{ g}\]

Final Answer: There would be $20.0 \text{ g}$ of Potassium-40 remaining.

Problem

This is the primary method geologists use to determine the age of rocks, allowing them to build a timeline of Earth's history.

A geologist finds a sample of zircon crystal. By analyzing its contents, she determines that 75% of the original Uranium-235 ($N_0$) has decayed into Lead-207. This means only 25% of the original Uranium-235 ($N(t)$) is left.

The half-life of Uranium-235 is 700 million years (or $7.0 \times 10^8$ years).

How old is the zircon crystal?

Given:

  • $N(t) = 0.25 \cdot N_0$ (25% remains)
  • $T_{1/2} = 700 \text{ million years}$

Find: $t$

Step 1: Identify Givens and Unknowns

  • $\frac{N(t)}{N_0} = 0.25$
  • $T_{1/2} = 700 \text{ million years}$
  • Unknown: $t = ?$

Step 2: Select Parent Equation

\[N(t) = N_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{t}{T_{1/2}}}\]

Step 3: Algebraic Rearrangement

First, divide by $N_0$: \(\frac{N(t)}{N_0} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{t}{T_{1/2}}}\)

Substitute the known ratio: \(0.25 = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{t}{T_{1/2}}}\)

Method 1: Logic We know that $0.25 = \frac{1}{4} = (\frac{1}{2})^2$. Therefore, the exponent must be 2. \(\frac{t}{T_{1/2}} = 2\) \(t = 2 \cdot T_{1/2} = 2 \cdot (700 \text{ million years}) = 1400 \text{ million years}\)

Method 2: Logarithms (for any ratio) \(\log(0.25) = \log\left[\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{t}{T_{1/2}}}\right]\) \(\log(0.25) = \left(\frac{t}{T_{1/2}}\right) \cdot \log(0.5)\) \(\frac{t}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{\log(0.25)}{\log(0.5)} = \frac{-0.602}{-0.301} = 2\) \(t = 2 \cdot (700 \text{ million years}) = 1400 \text{ million years}\)

Final Answer: The rock is 1400 million years old (or 1.4 billion years old).

Problem

This models how scientists would determine the half-life of a newly discovered isotope, a critical piece of data for understanding its potential as a heat source or a dating tool.

A sample of rock from the Afar Triangle is known to be very young, forming $t = 50,000 \text{ years}$ ago during a volcanic eruption.

Scientists analyze a new isotope, “Afar-99,” in the rock. They find that an initial amount of $N_0 = 64.0 \text{ \mu g}$ has decayed to $N(t) = 4.00 \text{ \mu g}$ in that time.

What is the half-life ($T_{1/2}$) of Afar-99?

Given:

  • $t = 50,000 \text{ years}$
  • $N_0 = 64.0 \text{ \mu g}$
  • $N(t) = 4.00 \text{ \mu g}$

Find: $T_{1/2}$

Step 1: Identify Givens and Unknowns

  • $t = 50,000 \text{ years}$
  • $N_0 = 64.0 \text{ \mu g}$
  • $N(t) = 4.00 \text{ \mu g}$
  • Unknown: $T_{1/2} = ?$

Step 2: Select Parent Equation

\[N(t) = N_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{t}{T_{1/2}}}\]

Step 3: Algebraic Rearrangement

First, find the ratio $\frac{N(t)}{N_0}$: \(\frac{4.00 \text{ \mu g}}{64.0 \text{ \mu g}} = 0.0625\)

Substitute into the equation: \(0.0625 = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{50,000}{T_{1/2}}}\)

Method 1: Logic Let’s find how many half-lives $n = \frac{t}{T_{1/2}}$ this is: $64.0 \xrightarrow{1} 32.0 \xrightarrow{2} 16.0 \xrightarrow{3} 8.00 \xrightarrow{4} 4.00$ It took 4 half-lives to decay to 4.00 \mu g.

So, $n = 4$. \(4 = \frac{t}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{50,000 \text{ years}}{T_{1/2}}\)

\[T_{1/2} = \frac{50,000 \text{ years}}{4} = 12,500 \text{ years}\]

Method 2: Logarithms \(\log(0.0625) = \left(\frac{50,000}{T_{1/2}}\right) \cdot \log(0.5)\) \(\frac{50,000}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{\log(0.0625)}{\log(0.5)} = \frac{-1.204}{-0.301} = 4.00\) \(T_{1/2} = \frac{50,000 \text{ years}}{4.00} = 12,500 \text{ years}\)

Final Answer: The half-life of Afar-99 is 12,500 years.
