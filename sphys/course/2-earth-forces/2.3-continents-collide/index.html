The Theory of Plate Tectonics

  • Core Idea: Earth’s rigid outer shell (the lithosphere - crust and solid upper mantle) is broken into several large tectonic plates.
  • These plates “float” or glide over a hotter, weaker, semi-molten layer below called the asthenosphere.
  • Driven primarily by mantle convection currents below (Lecture 2.2), these plates are in constant, slow motion (typically centimeters per year - as seen in Lab 2.1).
  • Most major geological activity (earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain building) occurs at the boundaries where these plates interact.

Types of Plate Boundaries

Plates interact at their edges in three fundamental ways:

  1. Divergent: Plates move apart $\leftarrow \rightarrow$ (Tension)
  2. Convergent: Plates move together $\rightarrow \leftarrow$ (Compression)
  3. Transform: Plates slide horizontally past each other $\rightleftarrows$ (Shear)

The type of boundary dictates the forces at play and the resulting geological features and hazards.

1. Divergent Boundaries ($\leftarrow \rightarrow$)

  • Motion: Plates pull apart.
  • Process: As plates separate, pressure decreases on the underlying mantle, causing it to partially melt and form magma. Magma rises to fill the gap, cools, and solidifies, creating new lithosphere (primarily oceanic crust).
  • Features:
    • Mid-Ocean Ridges: Long underwater mountain ranges formed by seafloor spreading (e.g., Mid-Atlantic Ridge). Characterized by shallow earthquakes and undersea volcanic activity. This process widens ocean basins.
    • Rift Valleys: Occur when divergence happens within a continent (e.g., East African Rift Valley, including our phenomenon in the Afar Triangle!). Characterized by faulting, volcanic activity, and shallow earthquakes. Can eventually lead to the continent splitting and forming a new ocean.

2. Convergent Boundaries ($\rightarrow \leftarrow$)

  • Motion: Plates collide. Old lithosphere is often recycled back into the mantle.
  • Process & Features (depend on crust types colliding):
    • Oceanic-Continental: Denser oceanic plate subducts (dives beneath) the lighter continental plate. The subducting plate heats up, releases water, causing mantle above it to melt. Magma rises.
      • Features: Deep ocean trench offshore, volcanic mountain range on the continent (e.g., Andes, Cascades), powerful earthquakes (shallow to deep).
    • Oceanic-Oceanic: One (usually older, colder, denser) oceanic plate subducts beneath another. Similar melting process occurs.
      • Features: Deep ocean trench, volcanic island arc chain parallel to the trench (e.g., Japan, Aleutian Islands, Mariana Islands), powerful earthquakes.
    • Continental-Continental: Neither plate subducts easily due to low density. Intense compression crumples and thickens the crust.
      • Features: Massive folded mountain ranges (non-volcanic or very limited volcanism, e.g., Himalayas, Alps), widespread shallow to intermediate earthquakes.

3. Transform Boundaries ($\rightleftarrows$)

  • Motion: Plates slide horizontally past each other along a transform fault.
  • Process: Lithosphere is neither created nor destroyed. Plates lock due to friction, storing elastic energy. When stress exceeds friction, the fault ruptures suddenly.
  • Features:
    • Fault Zones: Can be complex systems of parallel fractures (e.g., San Andreas Fault system in California). Visible offsets in landscape features (streams, fences).
    • Earthquakes: Often shallow but can be very destructive. No significant volcanic activity.

Connecting Boundaries to Global Patterns

  • Earthquake Distribution: The global map of earthquake epicenters acts like a “connect-the-dots” puzzle, precisely outlining the plate boundaries. Notice that deep earthquakes (markers shown deeper on maps) only occur at subduction zones (convergent boundaries), where plates are plunging deep into the mantle. Shallow earthquakes occur at all boundary types.
  • Volcano Distribution: Most of the world’s active volcanoes above sea level form arcs parallel to convergent (subduction) boundaries (the Pacific “Ring of Fire” is a prime example). Significant (though mostly unseen) volcanism also occurs along divergent boundaries (mid-ocean ridges and rift valleys). Transform boundaries generally lack volcanism.
  • The Rock Cycle Connection: Plate tectonics is the engine driving the rock cycle. Subduction pulls rock deep into the mantle for melting (-> igneous). Collision and uplift expose rock to weathering/erosion (-> sedimentary). Heat and pressure at boundaries transform existing rocks (-> metamorphic).

Answering Our Essential Questions

  1. Theory of Plate Tectonics?
    • Earth’s lithosphere is broken into rigid plates that move over the asthenosphere, driven by mantle convection. Activity occurs at boundaries.
  2. Three Main Boundary Types?
    • Divergent (apart $\leftarrow \rightarrow$), Convergent (together $\rightarrow \leftarrow$), Transform (slide past $\rightleftarrows$).
  3. Features of Each?
    • Divergent: Creates new crust (Mid-ocean ridges, rift valleys). Volcanic activity, shallow EQs.
    • Convergent: Destroys/deforms crust (Trenches, volcanic arcs, mountain ranges). Volcanic activity (if subduction), powerful EQs (shallow to deep).
    • Transform: Conserves crust (Faults). No volcanoes, shallow EQs.

Summary & Next Steps

Key Takeaways:

  • Plate tectonics explains the global patterns of earthquakes, volcanoes, and major landforms by the interactions at plate boundaries.
  • Each boundary type (divergent, convergent, transform) has characteristic processes and features.
