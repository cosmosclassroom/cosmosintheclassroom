5 Planetary Pulses

Chapter 5: A Planet’s Inertia and a Sudden Rip The Central Question: Why do things stay put, and what does it take to set a continent in motion?

Narrative Arc: We begin with the quiet stillness of the Earth’s surface, a stability so profound we take it for granted. This is the principle of inertia, the universe’s tendency to resist change. We then shatter this calm with the violent, ten-day tearing of the Afar Rift. This chapter frames the unit’s central mystery: What kind of force is powerful enough to overcome a continent’s inertia and tear it asunder?

5.1 A Crack in the World For most of human history, the ground beneath our feet has been the very definition of solid, stable, and permanent. Mountains rise and fall over timescales so vast they are nearly incomprehensible. Continents drift, but with a slowness that makes a glacier seem like a sprinter. We build our cities, our homes, our lives on the confident assumption that the Earth will, for the most part, stay put.

Then, on September 14, 2005, in the dusty Afar desert of Ethiopia, that assumption was shattered.

It began with a swarm of earthquakes. Then, the ground cracked open. In a geological instant—a mere ten days—a fissure 35 miles long and up to 26 feet wide ripped through the desert floor. It was as if the planet had unzipped itself. Volcanic vents hissed, spewing ash and magma. Scientists who rushed to the scene were stunned. They had witnessed the birth of what could one day be a new ocean, a process they thought took millions of years, accomplished in less than two weeks.

The Afar Rift is our new anchoring phenomenon. It is a profound and unsettling puzzle. It defies our everyday experience and forces us to ask a fundamental question: What could possibly do that? To even begin to answer this, we must first understand the opposite: why doesn’t this happen all the time? Why does the world, and everything in it, tend to stay put?

5.2 A Universe That Resists: The Law of Inertia Look at a book resting on your desk. It is not moving. It will, in all likelihood, remain exactly where it is until you or something else interacts with it. Now imagine a hockey puck gliding across a perfectly smooth sheet of ice, far from any friction. It will continue to glide in a straight line at a constant speed, seemingly forever, until it hits a wall or another player’s stick.

These two scenarios reveal a deep and fundamental property of the universe. Objects at rest tend to stay at rest, and objects in motion tend to stay in motion. This tendency to resist changes in motion is called inertia. It is a property of all matter, from the smallest atom to the largest galaxy.

In the 17th century, Isaac Newton formalized this observation into what we now know as Newton’s First Law of Motion, or the Law of Inertia. It states that an object will remain at rest or in uniform motion in a straight line unless acted upon by an external force.

A force is simply a push or a pull. It is the agent of change. A force is what you exert to lift the book from your desk. A force is what the hockey stick exerts to change the puck’s direction. To understand why the Afar Rift happened, we must be looking for a force—an unimaginably large one, capable of overcoming the inertia of an entire continent.

5.3 The Unseen Tug of War: Balanced and Unbalanced Forces If you and a friend push on opposite sides of a box with equal force, the box doesn’t move. You are both applying forces, but they cancel each other out. This is a state of balanced forces. The net force—the sum of all forces acting on the object—is zero. When forces are balanced, there is no change in motion. The box stays at rest, obeying the law of inertia. The ground beneath your feet is, right now, in a state of (mostly) balanced forces, with the downward pull of gravity perfectly countered by the upward push of the rock below it. This is the Stability part of the “Stability and Change” thinking lens.

But what happens if another friend joins in and pushes with you? Now the forces are unequal. They are unbalanced. The net force is no longer zero, and the box begins to move. It accelerates in the direction of the stronger push. This is the Change.

For the Afar Rift to have occurred, a previously balanced system of forces deep within the Earth must have suddenly become unbalanced. For millennia, the forces holding the African tectonic plate together were in equilibrium with the forces trying to pull it apart. Then, in September 2005, the pulling force must have become dramatically, catastrophically greater than the forces holding the rock together. The result was a rupture—a rapid, violent change in the state of motion of the Earth’s crust. Our challenge is to become detectives, using the laws of physics to uncover the source of that titanic, unbalanced force.

5.4 Pause and Model: Your First Ideas We now have the first tools of our investigation: inertia tells us that the continent should have stayed put, and the concept of force tells us that an unbalanced push or pull must be the culprit.

This is a key moment in any scientific inquiry—the point where we stop gathering initial data and try to form a first hypothesis. The science practice of Developing and Using Models is not about being right on the first try; it is about making our current thinking visible so that we can test it, critique it, and improve it as we gather more evidence.

Take some time to think like a geologist and a physicist. Based only on what you know right now, what could have caused such a massive unbalanced force?
