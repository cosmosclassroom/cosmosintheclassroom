6 Plate Force Motion

Chapter 6: The Unseen Engine: Quantifying Force and Change The Central Question: If a force caused the rift, how can we measure the relationship between that push, the mass of the tectonic plates, and the speed of the rupture?

Narrative Arc: To move from mystery to explanation, we need a rulebook for the universe. This chapter introduces the grand equation of motion, F=ma, as the Rosetta Stone for translating the invisible cause (force) into the visible effect (acceleration). We will treat the Earth’s crust as a system of objects and learn the quantitative tools needed to analyze the immense forces at play.

6.1 From ‘Why’ to ‘How Much’ In the last chapter, we reached a critical conclusion: an unbalanced force must have torn the Afar desert apart. This is a powerful, but incomplete, explanation. It gives us the why, but not the how much. Was the force equivalent to a million locomotives pulling at once? A billion? A trillion?

To answer this, we need to find a measurable link between the force applied and the change in motion it produces. That measurable change is acceleration. Acceleration is not just about speeding up; it is any change in an object’s velocity—speeding up, slowing down, or changing direction. The swerving hockey puck, the book as you begin to lift it, and the tectonic plates as they began to rip apart were all accelerating.

Acceleration is the visible fingerprint left behind by an unbalanced force. If we can measure the acceleration of an object, and we know its inertia, we can calculate the exact size of the force that set it in motion. This moves us from a qualitative story to a quantitative investigation, which is the heart of physics.

Check for Understanding

What is the difference between velocity and acceleration? Give an example of an object that is accelerating but not speeding up.

Why is being able to calculate the size of the force important for solving the Afar Rift mystery?

6.2 The Great Equation of Motion: F=ma How exactly are force, inertia, and acceleration related? This question obsessed Isaac Newton. Through meticulous observation and brilliant insight, he discovered a relationship of stunning simplicity and universal power. It is perhaps the most important single equation in all of classical physics: Newton’s Second Law of Motion.

The law states that the acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass.

In the language of mathematics, it is written:

F net ​ =ma

Where:

F_net is the net force acting on the object (measured in Newtons, N). This is the sum of all pushes and pulls.

m is the mass of the object (measured in kilograms, kg). This is the quantitative measure of its inertia—how much it resists changes in motion.

a is the acceleration of the object (measured in meters per second squared, m/s²).

This equation is our engine of inquiry. It means that if you push twice as hard on an object (double the F_net), it will accelerate twice as much. If you push with the same force on an object with twice the mass (double the m), it will accelerate only half as much. It is an elegant, perfect balance between cause (force) and effect (acceleration), moderated by the object’s inherent resistance to change (mass).

Check for Understanding

You push a small car and a large truck with the exact same force. Which one accelerates more, and why? Use F=ma to explain.

A 10 kg rock is accelerating at 2 m/s². What is the net force acting on it?

6.3 The Burden of Being: Mass vs. Weight In everyday language, we often use the words “mass” and “weight” interchangeably. In physics, they are fundamentally different, and understanding this difference is crucial to using Newton’s Second Law correctly.

Mass (m) is the amount of “stuff” an object is made of. It is the measure of inertia we discussed. Your mass is the same whether you are on Earth, on the Moon, or floating in deep space. It is an intrinsic property of you.

Weight (W) is the force of gravity acting on an object’s mass. It is a force, measured in Newtons. Your weight depends entirely on where you are. On the Moon, where gravity is weaker, you would weigh only one-sixth of what you do on Earth, even though your mass hasn’t changed at all.

Weight is calculated using Newton’s Second Law, where the force is weight (W) and the acceleration is the acceleration due to gravity (g), which is approximately 9.8 m/s² on Earth’s surface.

W=mg

When we stand on the ground, the downward force of our weight is perfectly balanced by the upward force of the ground pushing back on us. The net force is zero, so we don’t accelerate. For the Afar Rift to open, the forces tearing the crust apart had to overcome not just the rock’s immense inertia, but also the incredible forces holding it together against the constant pull of gravity.

Check for Understanding

An astronaut has a mass of 80 kg. What is her weight on Earth? What is her mass on the Moon?

A book is resting on a table. Is there a net force acting on it? Explain in terms of balanced forces and F=ma.

6.4 The Unseen Brake: The Force of Friction Our investigation of the Afar Rift now has a powerful engine (F net ​ =ma), but we are missing one key element of the real world: resistance. Tectonic plates are not gliding on perfectly smooth ice. They are grinding against each other, a system filled with incredible friction.

Friction is a force that opposes motion (or attempted motion) between surfaces in contact. It is the unseen brake that always acts against the direction of movement. When we calculate the net force, we must always account for friction. If you are pushing a box to the right with a force of 100 N and friction is pushing back to the left with a force of 20 N, the net force is not 100 N, but rather:

F net ​ =F push ​ −F friction ​ =100N−20N=80N

It is this 80 N net force that determines the box’s acceleration. For the African continent to be ripped apart, the geological forces pulling it open had to be greater than the immense static friction holding the tectonic plate together. The 2005 event suggests that for a brief, violent period, the driving force overwhelmed the frictional force, resulting in a large net force and a terrifyingly rapid acceleration. Our investigation must now turn to the heart of the Earth to find the source of that driving force.

Check for Understanding

You are sliding a heavy box across the floor at a constant velocity. Is there a net force on the box? What can you say about the force of your push compared to the force of friction?

How does the concept of friction add a layer of complexity to the mystery of the Afar Rift?
