Chapter 11: The Unbreakable Rule: Conservation of Momentum

Central Question: In the chaos of a collision, is there anything that stays the same? Is there a rule that even the most violent crash must obey?

Narrative Arc: We have explored how forces change the momentum of a single object. Now, we zoom out to discover a law that governs the interaction between objects. We will find that while momentum can be transferred from one object to another, it can never be created or destroyed. This is the Law of Conservation of Momentum, an unbreakable rule of the universe that allows accident investigators to reconstruct the past and physicists to predict the future.

11.1 Defining the System

Before we can state the law, we must define our terms. In physics, a system is a collection of objects that we are interested in studying. For a collision between two cars, our system consists of Car A and Car B.

The forces that the cars exert on each other during the collision are called internal forces. Forces exerted by things outside the system—like friction from the road or air resistance—are called external forces.

The Law of Conservation of Momentum applies to any closed, isolated system—one where there are no significant external forces acting on it. While a car crash isn’t perfectly isolated (there’s always some friction), the forces the cars exert on each other are so enormous that, for the brief moment of the collision, we can often ignore the external forces.

Thinking Lens (Systems and System Models): Why is defining the boundaries of your system the essential first step before you can analyze an interaction? What would happen to our analysis if we defined our system as ‘just Car A’ instead of ‘Car A and Car B’?

11.2 The Law: What Goes In Must Come Out

The Law of Conservation of Momentum is elegant and profound: In a closed, isolated system, the total momentum of all objects before a collision is equal to the total momentum of all objects after the collision.

Momentum is not lost; it is simply transferred between objects. The math is straightforward:

\[\Sigma p_{initial} = \Sigma p_{final}\]

Think of a billiard ball collision. Before the collision, the moving cue ball has momentum, and the stationary 8-ball has zero momentum. After the collision, both balls are moving. The cue ball has transferred some of its momentum to the 8-ball, but if you were to add up the individual momenta of both balls after the crash, the total would be exactly the same as the initial momentum of the cue ball. The universe keeps a perfect accounting.

11.3 Bounces and Splats: Elastic vs. Inelastic Collisions

While total momentum is always conserved, the fate of the system’s kinetic energy—the energy of motion—depends on the nature of the collision.

  • Inelastic Collision: This is a collision where the objects deform, make sound, or generate heat. Kinetic energy is not conserved because it is transformed into these other forms. Most real-world collisions, including car crashes, are inelastic. If two cars collide and stick together, it is a perfectly inelastic collision. The mangled metal is physical evidence of the kinetic energy that was converted into the work of deforming the cars.

  • Elastic Collision: This is a “perfect” bounce where the objects rebound without any loss of kinetic energy. While no real-world collision is perfectly elastic, the collision between billiard balls or air hockey pucks are very close approximations.

11.4 Reconstructing the Crash

The Law of Conservation of Momentum is a powerful forensic tool. After a crash, investigators can measure the final mass and velocity of the vehicles (from skid marks and resting positions). They can then use the equation $\Sigma p_{initial} = \Sigma p_{final}$ to work backward and calculate the initial velocities of the cars before the crash.

This allows them to determine if a driver was speeding or violating other traffic laws. It is a perfect example of using a fundamental law of physics to reconstruct a past event and find a clear, evidence-based answer. It is how we know what happened, even when we weren’t there to see it.
