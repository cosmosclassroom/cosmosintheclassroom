14.1 The Paradox of the Orbit

It is a deep and important puzzle. We know the Earth pulls on the Moon with an immense gravitational force. Why hasn’t the Moon crashed into the Earth? What keeps it suspended in the sky? The secret ingredient is not an upward force, but a sideways one. The Moon is constantly moving forward at a tremendous speed. Without gravity, it would fly off in a straight line into deep space. Without its forward motion, it would plummet directly to Earth. The combination of these two motions—a forward velocity and a downward acceleration due to gravity—is what creates the stable, curved path of an orbit.

14.2 Newton's Cannonball: Falling and Missing

To visualize this, Isaac Newton imagined a giant cannon on top of a very

high mountain.

  • If you fire the cannonball with a low speed, it travels a short distance

before gravity pulls it to the ground. - If you fire it with a higher speed,

it travels much farther before crashing. - If you could fire it at a very specific,

incredibly high speed, something amazing happens. As the cannonball falls,

the Earth’s surface curves away beneath it at the exact same rate. The cannonball

is constantly falling towards the Earth, but it is moving so fast sideways that

it never gets any closer. It is “falling around” the planet.

This is what an orbit is: the art of falling and consistently missing the ground.

Every satellite, the International Space Station, and the Moon itself are all

in a state of freefall, captured by this elegant balance.

14.3 The Energy of an Orbit: A Cosmic Bank Account

An orbit isn’t just a balance of motion; it’s also a balance of energy.

An orbiting object has a “cosmic bank account” with two types of funds:

  • Kinetic Energy (KE): The energy of motion. The faster an object moves, the

more kinetic energy it has. ($KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$) - **Gravitational Potential

Energy (PE):** The energy of position. The farther an object is from the central

body, the more potential energy it has, because it has farther to fall.

In a perfectly circular orbit, both KE and PE remain constant. But most orbits,

like that of the Chelyabinsk meteor, are elliptical. In an elliptical orbit, the

object is constantly trading one form of energy for the other. As it moves farther

away, it slows down (losing KE, gaining PE). As it swings back and gets closer,

gravity pulls it in, causing it to speed up (losing PE, gaining KE). The *total

energy* (KE + PE) of the orbit, however, remains the same. This is the law of

conservation of energy at a cosmic scale.
