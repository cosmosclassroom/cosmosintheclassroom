Introduction: The Solar System's Scrap Metal

We have established that the solar system is a place of elegant, stable orbits governed by the universal law of gravitation. However, this beautiful clockwork is not perfect. The space between the planets is filled with leftover debris from the formation of the solar system—asteroids and comets of all sizes, each following its own orbital path. Inevitably, some of these paths cross.

The Question: How do we read the history of past collisions, and how does that history inform our understanding of the future threat?

15.1 The Scars of the Moon

The most obvious evidence of cosmic impacts is written on the face of the Moon. With no atmosphere to burn up incoming meteors and no weather to erode the surface, the Moon has preserved a pristine record of the solar system’s violent history. Every one of the millions of craters you can see through a telescope is a scar from a past collision.

The Earth has been hit just as often, if not more often, than the Moon. Our planet is simply much better at hiding its scars. Weathering, erosion, and the constant recycling of the Earth’s crust through plate tectonics have erased all but the most recent and significant impact sites. But the evidence is there, if you know how to look for it.

15.2 The Dinosaur Killer: The Chicxulub Impact

About 66 million years ago, the reign of the dinosaurs came to an abrupt and catastrophic end. The culprit was a Near-Earth Object estimated to be at least 6 miles (10 kilometers) in diameter. It struck the Earth in what is now the Yucatán Peninsula in Mexico.

The impact was cataclysmic. It released more energy than a billion atomic bombs, creating a fireball that incinerated everything for hundreds of miles. It triggered global earthquakes and tsunamis, and threw so much dust and debris into the atmosphere that it blocked out the sun for years, causing a global winter that led to the extinction of 75% of all species on Earth, including the non-avian dinosaurs.

The evidence for this event is a thin layer of iridium—an element rare on Earth but common in asteroids—found in rock strata all over the world, dating to precisely the same time. At the center of it all is the massive, 110-mile-wide Chicxulub crater, buried beneath layers of sediment.

15.3 The Modern Threat: Planetary Defense

Today, astronomers around the world are engaged in a massive effort to find, track, and characterize Near-Earth Objects. Programs like NASA’s Planetary Defense Coordination Office work to identify any asteroid that could pose a threat long before it becomes a danger.

The good news is that we have now cataloged over 95% of the truly large, dinosaur-killer-sized asteroids, and none of them pose a threat for the foreseeable future. The remaining challenge is finding the smaller, city-killer-sized objects like the one that exploded over Chelyabinsk. Because there are so many of them, and they are harder to detect, they represent the most probable, if still unlikely, near-term risk.
