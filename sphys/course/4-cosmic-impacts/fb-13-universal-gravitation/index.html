13.1 A Stone from the Sky

On the morning of February 15, 2013, the citizens of Chelyabinsk, Russia,

witnessed a terrifying spectacle. A streak of light, brighter than the sun, tore

across the winter sky. It was not a plane or a missile. It was a rock, an asteroid

about 20 meters in diameter, that had been traveling through space for millions

of years. It entered Earth’s atmosphere at over 40,000 miles per hour.

The immense pressure and heat caused the asteroid to explode in a brilliant fireball

nearly 18 miles above the ground. The resulting shockwave, arriving minutes later,

blasted out windows, damaged thousands of buildings, and injured over 1,500 people.

It was the largest known object to strike the Earth in over a century, and it

was a stark reminder that we live in a cosmic shooting gallery. This event is

our new anchoring phenomenon. Why did it happen? And could it happen again?

13.2 Newton's Great Idea: The Universal Tug

We are used to gravity. It’s the force that pulls a dropped apple to

the ground. But Isaac Newton’s genius was to realize that the same force pulling

the apple also reached far, far higher—all the way to the Moon. He proposed that

gravity was not just a property of the Earth, but a universal force. Every

object with mass in the universe pulls on every other object with mass. You are

gravitationally attracted to your desk, the person next to you, and the Andromeda

galaxy.

The force that pulled the Chelyabinsk meteor toward Earth is the very same force

that keeps the Moon in orbit and holds the Sun’s family of planets together.

To analyze cosmic collisions, we need a law that works on this grand, universal

scale.

13.3 The Law of Universal Gravitation

Newton formulated this universal tug with a single, powerful equation

that describes the force of gravity ($F_g$) between any two objects:

\[F_g = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}\]

Let’s break this down: - $m_1$ and $m_2$ are the masses of the two objects.

The more massive the objects, the stronger the pull. - $r$ is the distance

between the centers of the two objects. - $G$ is the Universal Gravitational

Constant, a tiny number that shows why gravity is the weakest of the fundamental

forces.

The most important part of this law is the inverse-square relationship. The

force of gravity decreases with the square of the distance. If you double the

distance between two objects, the gravitational force between them doesn’t get

twice as weak; it gets four times weaker ($2^2 = 4$). This is why the pull

of distant stars on Earth is negligible, while the pull of our nearby Sun and

Moon dominates our cosmic neighborhood.
