Overview

Abstract

In this final investigation, you will act as a scientifically literate citizen. Your task is to use a scientific model of the electromagnetic (EM) spectrum to critically evaluate a series of real-world claims made about the safety of 5G technology. By comparing the claims to the known properties of different types of radiation, you will construct an evidence-based argument to either support or refute them.

Introduction

New technologies often bring public concern, and the rollout of 5G cellular networks has been surrounded by a storm of claims and misinformation about its potential health effects. The word “radiation” itself can be scary, but as we have learned, not all radiation is the same. The key to navigating this information landscape is to apply a scientific model. Your job is not to decide whether 5G is “good” or “bad,” but to practice using scientific evidence to evaluate claims and separate fact from fiction.

Investigation : Investigation: Fact-Checking with the EM Spectrum

You will be provided with two key assets:

  1. A detailed diagram of the electromagnetic spectrum. This model shows the location of different types of radiation (radio, microwave, visible, UV, X-ray) and indicates their relative frequency, wavelength, and energy. It will also clearly mark the dividing line between non-ionizing and ionizing radiation.
  2. A collection of curated text snippets. These include headlines, social media posts, and short article excerpts that make various claims about 5G radiation.

Connection to Unit Phenomenon

This lab connects our understanding of the microwave oven to a complex, real-world societal issue. The very same type of radiation used to heat food in our anchoring phenomenon—microwaves—is used for 5G communications. By understanding the physics, we know that microwaves transfer energy by causing water molecules to rotate (creating heat), but they are non-ionizing and do not have the energy to cause the kind of cellular damage associated with high-frequency radiation. This lab demonstrates that scientific literacy is not just about understanding phenomena, but about using established scientific models to be an informed and critical consumer of information in a technologically complex world.
