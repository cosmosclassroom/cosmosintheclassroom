▶ Key Vocabulary (4 terms)
Big Bang Theory

The leading scientific theory for how the universe began, stating that it started from an initial state of incredibly high density and temperature and has been expanding ever since.

Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB)

A faint glow of electromagnetic radiation, primarily in the microwave range, that fills the entire universe and is the remnant heat from the Big Bang.

Redshift

The stretching of light waves to longer wavelengths as the source of the light moves away from the observer. A key piece of evidence for the expanding universe.

Hubble's Law

The observation that galaxies are moving away from the Earth at speeds proportional to their distance. It implies the universe is expanding uniformly.

▶ Key Formulae (1 formulas)

$v = H_0 d$

Hubble’s Law, where a galaxy’s recessional velocity (v) is equal to its distance (d) times the Hubble Constant ($H_0$).

Introduction: The Final Mystery

Our journey through physics has taken us from the circuits in our walls to the forces that shape planets and the invisible waves that carry our messages. We have learned a set of universal rules that govern energy and motion. For our final chapter, we turn these tools to the biggest question of all: Where did everything come from?

The answer, it turns out, was hidden in a faint, persistent hiss in the microwave part of the very spectrum we just studied. It is the story of the greatest detective case in the history of science.

The Question: Why is the entire universe, in every direction we look, glowing with a faint bath of microwave radiation?

18.1 The Persistent Hum of the Universe

In 1964, two American radio astronomers, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, were trying to use a large radio antenna to study our galaxy. They were plagued by a persistent, low-level noise—a steady, uniform hiss of microwave radiation that seemed to come from everywhere at once. It was the same no matter where they pointed their antenna, day or night, season after season. They checked their equipment, rewired their systems, and even cleaned out pigeon droppings from the horn of the antenna, but the hiss remained.

They had accidentally stumbled upon the most important discovery in modern cosmology: the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB). This faint glow wasn’t a flaw in their equipment or an earthly signal; it was a feature of the universe itself. But what was it?

18.2 A Universe in Flight

The first major clue to solving this puzzle had been found decades earlier by the astronomer Edwin Hubble. While observing distant galaxies in the 1920s, he noticed something astonishing. He analyzed the light from these galaxies and saw that its wavelengths were stretched, or redshifted. Just as the pitch of an ambulance siren drops as it moves away from you (the Doppler effect), the wavelength of light from a galaxy stretches to the red end of the spectrum as it moves away from us.

Hubble discovered that nearly every galaxy is redshifted, meaning they are all moving away from us. Even more profound, he found a clear pattern: the farther away a galaxy is, the faster it is moving away. This became known as Hubble’s Law.

18.3 The Inescapable Conclusion

If all galaxies are rushing away from each other, what happens if we run the clock backward? The conclusion is inescapable. In the past, everything in the universe must have been closer together. If we go back far enough, roughly 13.8 billion years ago, everything—all matter, all energy—must have been compressed into a single, infinitesimally small, incredibly hot, and dense point.

This is the origin of the Big Bang Theory. It wasn’t an “explosion” in the traditional sense, but the simultaneous expansion of space itself, everywhere, from this initial hot, dense state.

18.4 The Echo of Creation

Now we can solve the puzzle. If the universe began in an unimaginably hot fireball, it must have been glowing with intense, high-energy radiation. For the first few hundred thousand years, this light was trapped in a hot plasma. But as the universe expanded and cooled, a moment came when the light was finally set free to travel across the cosmos.

Over the next 13.8 billion years, the continued expansion of space has stretched that primordial light. Its wavelength has been elongated from high-energy gamma rays, down through the spectrum, all the way to the low-energy microwave radiation we see today.

The hiss that Penzias and Wilson discovered was not noise. It was the afterglow of creation. The Cosmic Microwave Background is the oldest light in the universe, a fossil relic of the Big Bang, bathing the cosmos in the faint, residual heat from its own birth.
