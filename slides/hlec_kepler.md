# History

- Our ancient ancestors noticed that certain “stars” did not behave like the others.
- Against a backdrop of stars that always rose in the east and set in the west, five were seen to break their paths and, for a time, drift backward or even pause.
- The Greeks called these objects “planets” (Greek for wanderer).
- They explained their irregular behavior as acts of volition and associated them with powerful gods and goddesses.

---

![bg left drop-shadow](<img/5_circ-gravitation/0 Lecture _ 53 Laws of Planetary Motion_1.jpg>)

- Ptolemy’s Earth-centered (geocentric) model of the solar system held sway for nearly 1,400 years.
- Ptolemy (and Aristotle) taught of a perfect, unchanging universe.
- The heavens were governed by the laws of geometry, and all orbits were perfect circles.

---
# Brahe
<!-- _class: invert -->
- In 1576, Tycho Brahe became annoyed with the quality of astronomical observations, some of which had been made by Ptolemy himself.
![alt text](<img/5_circ-gravitation/0 Lecture _ 53 Laws of Planetary Motion_11.png>)

- Tycho noted the appearance of a star that was brighter than any other and which had not been previously recorded.
- He called this new star a *stellae novae* (now supernova), refuting Plato and Aristotle’s idea that the heavens were unchanging.

## Equal areas in equal time

- Kepler’s Second Law states that planets sweep out equal areas in equal time as they orbit.

![bg 50% left](<img/5_circ-gravitation/0 Lecture _ 53 Laws of Planetary Motion_6.png>)

- The time taken to travel this distance is equal to the time taken to travel this distance.
- Thus the object must speed up and slow down as it travels.

---
## Imperfect circles

- Kepler’s Third Law states that the ratio of the squares of the distances between revolving planets is equal to the ratio of the cubes of their mean distances from the star (the semi-major axis of the ellipse). 

$$ \left(\frac{T_1}{T_2}) $$

![bg 50% left](<img/5_circ-gravitation/0 Lecture _ 53 Laws of Planetary Motion_7.png>)

- _s_ is often used to indicate average distance here.

![bg 50% left](<img/5_circ-gravitation/0 Lecture _ 53 Laws of Planetary Motion_8.png>)

- This only applies to two objects moving around the same parent body.

# Units in Kepler’s Laws

- This is always an average distance through the orbit.
- Kepler’s Third Law is described as a proportion, so we often calculate it proportionally to Earth’s orbit.

![bg 50% left](<img/5_circ-gravitation/0 Lecture _ 53 Laws of Planetary Motion_9.png>)

![bg 50% left](<img/5_circ-gravitation/0 Lecture _ 53 Laws of Planetary Motion_10.png>)

- Period, _T_, is measured in _Earth years_ (Earth’s orbital period).
- Distance, _s_, is given in astronomical units (AU), or the average distance between Earth’s center and the center of the Sun.
- 1 AU = ~150,000,000 km or 1.5 x 10^8 km.

---
# Distances and periods

- One advantage of using these units is that they simplify the proportion.
- Meaning that if a planet’s orbital period or average distance is known, the other can be calculated (somewhat) simply.

# Kepler’s Third Law in our Solar System

- $$\left(\frac{T_{planet}}{T_{Earth}}\right)^2 \propto \left(\frac{s_{planet}}{s_{Earth}}\right)^3 $$
- $$\left(\frac{T_{planet}}{1\, year}\right)^2 \propto \left(\frac{s_{planet}}{1\,AU}\right)^3 $$
- $$ (T_{planet})^2 \propto (s_{planet})^3  $$

# Newton’s Synthesis

- Our calculations will ignore the gravitational effects of the planets on each other (they are very small).
- Kepler’s Third Law helped provide a theoretical basis that confirmed Newton’s Universal Law of Gravitation.

![bg 50% left](<img/5_circ-gravitation/0 Lecture _ 53 Laws of Planetary Motion_16.png>)

- Newton’s Universal Law predicted this.
- Slight changes to the orbits over time would be expected to be observed when the gravity of each planet was accounted for.
