Introduction

Flexbook Chapter 9: Work and Energy

learning_objectives:

  • Define work in the physics context and calculate the work done by a constant force.
  • Define kinetic energy and apply the work-energy theorem to relate the net work done on an object to its change in kinetic energy.
  • Differentiate between various forms of potential energy, with a focus on calculating gravitational potential energy.

keyVocabulary:

  • term: Work definition: >- The measure of energy transfer that occurs when an object is moved over a distance by an external force.
  • term: Kinetic Energy definition: >- The energy that an object possesses due to its motion, dependent on its mass and speed.
  • term: Potential Energy definition: >- Stored energy that depends upon the relative position of various parts of a system. ($PE_g$ is energy stored due to height).
  • term: Work-Energy Theorem definition: >- The principle that the net work done on an object is equal to the change in its kinetic energy.

keyFormulae:

  • formula: $W = Fd\cos(\theta)$
  • formula: $KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$
  • formula: $W_{net} = \Delta KE$
  • formula: $PE_g = mgh$

  • type: reading title: “Introduction: A New Way of Keeping Score” content: | For the past several chapters, we’ve been like accountants for motion, meticulously tracking the pushes and pulls—the forces—that cause acceleration. But what if there’s another, more powerful way to understand the universe? Imagine a universal currency, a quantity that can change its form from motion, to position, to heat, but whose total amount is always conserved. This currency is called energy. By learning to track it, we can solve problems that are incredibly difficult using forces alone. This is not just a new tool; it’s a new worldview. It’s the story of how physicists learned to stop focusing only on the transactions of force and started balancing the universe’s checkbook.

  • type: reading title: “Phenomenon: A River Tamed, A Desert Bloomed” content: |

    In the heart of the Great Depression, a monumental structure of concrete and steel rose from the Black Canyon of the Colorado River. The Hoover Dam, a testament to human ambition, tamed a wild river to bring life and power to the American Southwest. The core principle is deceptively simple: water held high behind the dam wall possesses a quiet, stored energy. As this water is released, that stored energy is transformed into the violent, churning energy of motion. This motion turns massive turbines, which transform the energy of motion into the electrical energy that powers cities. The dam is a colossal energy converter. The water behind it isn’t just a reservoir; it’s a battery, storing the energy gifted by the sun that lifted the water from the ocean in the first place. The puzzle is: how do we create a precise accounting system to track this energy as it changes from one form to another?

  • type: reading title: “The Rules of the Game: Work and Energy” content: | Your investigation revealed the rule for “doing work”: $W = Fd\cos(\theta)$. Work is the product of the force and the distance over which it’s applied, but only the component of the force in the direction of motion counts (that’s what the $\cos\theta$ is for). If you push a box horizontally, you do work. If you just hold it still, no matter how tired you get, you are doing zero work because the distance is zero.

    So where does that transferred energy go? It often becomes Kinetic Energy ($KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$), the energy an object has because it’s moving. The link between these is the Work-Energy Theorem: the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy ($W_{net} = \Delta KE$). This is a powerful idea. It tells us that the total of all pushes and pulls on an object, accounted for over a distance, precisely determines its change in speed.

    But energy can also be stored. When you lift an object, you do work against gravity. That energy isn’t lost; it’s stored as Gravitational Potential Energy ($PE_g = mgh$). It’s the energy of position—the higher an object is, the more potential it has to be converted back into motion. This is the secret of the Hoover Dam: storing energy by putting water in a high place.

  • type: reading title: “Thinking Lens: Energy and Matter” content: | Using our lens of Energy and Matter, we see that energy is a fundamental quantity that can be tracked as it flows through a system. The Hoover Dam is a perfect example of a system designed to manage energy flow. The matter (water) enters the system at a high potential energy. The structure of the dam guides the flow of this matter, causing the energy to be transformed into kinetic, then mechanical, then electrical forms. The matter itself is just the carrier for the energy. This lens encourages us to stop looking at just the objects and start looking at the energy they carry, store, and transfer. The total amount of energy may change form, but it is never lost from the universe.

  • type: reading title: “The Bigger Picture: Synthesis and Connection” content: | The concepts of work and energy give us a profoundly new way to analyze the physical world. Instead of summing up vector forces, we can now track a single scalar quantity—energy—as it changes form. We have laid the groundwork for one of the most important and sacrosanct laws in all of science: the Law of Conservation of Energy.
