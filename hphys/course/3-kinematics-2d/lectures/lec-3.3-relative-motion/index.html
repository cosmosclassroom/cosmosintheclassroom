Relative Velocity: A Matter of Perspective

Lecture 3.3: A Matter of Perspective

Unit 3: The World in Two Dimensions

Today’s Mission

  • Bridge Back: So far, we have described motion from a single, fixed point of view: the ground. But what if the observer is also moving? What if our cannon were mounted on a moving warship?
  • Thinking Lens: We will use Systems and System Models to understand that the description of motion depends entirely on the chosen frame of reference.
  • Essential Questions:
    • What is a “frame of reference” and why is it the most important first step in describing motion?
    • How do we use vector addition to find an object’s velocity relative to a different observer?

The Core Idea: All Motion is Relative

Imagine you are on a perfectly smooth train with no windows. You toss a ball straight up. To you, it goes straight up and straight down.

But to an observer on the ground, the ball is also moving forward at the speed of the train. They see the ball follow a parabolic arc, just like a projectile.

Who is correct? You both are.

Motion is not absolute. It can only be described relative to a frame of reference (the observer’s point of view).

Relative Velocity in One Dimension

Let’s start simple. A passenger walks forward at $2\,\text{m/s}$ on a train that is moving at $30\,\text{m/s}$. How fast is the passenger moving relative to the ground?

We use a special subscript notation to keep track of the frames of reference:

  • $v_{PG}$ = velocity of the Passenger relative to the Ground
  • $v_{PT}$ = velocity of the Passenger relative to the Train
  • $v_{TG}$ = velocity of the Train relative to the Ground

The rule is: \(v_{AC} = v_{AB} + v_{BC}\)

So, \(v_{PG} = v_{PT} + v_{TG} = 2\,\text{m/s} + 30\,\text{m/s} = 32\,\text{m/s}\)

What if the passenger walks backward? Then $v_{PT} = -2\,\text{m/s}$, so $v_{PG} = -2\,\text{m/s} + 30\,\text{m/s} = 28\,\text{m/s}$.

Relative Velocity in Two Dimensions

This is where it gets interesting. The vector addition rule still holds, but now we must add the vectors tip-to-tail, just like in Unit 1.

The Classic Problem: A boat wants to travel straight across a river.

  • The boat’s engine can propel it at $4.0\,\text{m/s}$ relative to the water ($v_{BW}$).
  • The river flows downstream at $3.0\,\text{m/s}$ relative to the ground ($v_{WG}$).

What is the boat’s actual velocity relative to the ground ($v_{BG}$)?

Worked Example: Crossing the River

We need to find $v_{BG} = v_{BW} + v_{WG}$.

This is a vector addition problem. We add the two velocity vectors tip-to-tail to find the resultant vector.

Magnitude (Speed): Use the Pythagorean theorem. \(|v_{BG}|^2 = |v_{BW}|^2 + |v_{WG}|^2\) \(|v_{BG}| = \sqrt{(4.0\,\text{m/s})^2 + (3.0\,\text{m/s})^2} = 5.0\,\text{m/s}\)

Direction (Angle): Use trigonometry. \(\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{4.0}{3.0}\right) = 53^\circ\)

The boat moves at $5.0\,\text{m/s}$ at an angle of $53^\circ$ downstream from its intended path.

Check for Understanding

An airplane pilot wants to fly her plane due north. She knows there is a strong wind blowing due east.

In which direction must she point the nose of the airplane to actually travel due north?

A) Due north
B) Due east
C) Northeast
D) Northwest

Answer: D) Northwest. She must aim the plane partially into the wind (to the west) to cancel out the wind’s eastward push.

Answering Our Essential Questions

1. What is a “frame of reference” and why is it important?

A frame of reference is the perspective from which motion is observed.

It is crucial because the description of an object’s velocity can be different for different observers. There is no single “correct” answer without first defining the frame of reference.

2. How do we find an object’s velocity relative to a different observer?

We use vector addition: \(v_{AC} = v_{AB} + v_{BC}\)

In 1D, this is simple addition or subtraction.

In 2D, we must add the velocity vectors tip-to-tail using the Pythagorean theorem and trigonometry.

Summary and Next Steps

The Core Idea:
What you see depends on where you stand. An object’s velocity is not absolute; it’s a combination of its own motion and the motion of its environment.
