Unit 3: The World in Two Dimensions

Anchoring Phenomenon:
The first effective use of large-scale artillery in the 1453 Siege of Constantinople.

Overarching Question:
How can we analyze complex, two-dimensional motion as the sum of simple, independent linear motions?

Part 1: Introducing Two-Dimensional Motion

Lecture 3.1: The Independence of Motion
Core Idea: Introduce the fundamental principle that the horizontal and vertical components of motion are independent of each other. This lecture sets the stage by connecting this idea to the trajectory of a cannonball.

Prepares For:

  • Problem Set 3.1: Introduction to Projectile Motion

Flexbook Reading:

  • Projectile Motion
    • “Vector Components for Projectile Motion”
    • “Solving Projectile Motion Problems - Launched Horizontally”

Hexagon Lab:

  • Analyzing Projectile Motion Data
    • Objective: Review simulated launch data to draw conclusions about launch angles, initial velocities, and displacements.

Problem Set 3.1:

  • Introduction to Projectile Motion
    • Focus: Applying the concepts of independent motion, specifically for objects launched horizontally.

Part 2: Angled Projectiles and Optimization

Lecture 3.2: Angled Projectiles and Maximum Range
Core Idea: Builds on the previous lecture by introducing vector components (trigonometry) to analyze projectiles launched at an angle.

Prepares For:

  • Problem Set 3.2: Angled Projectile Motion

Hexagon Lab:

  • Optimizing Launch Angle for Range
    • Objective: Use a simulation to investigate the relationship between launch angle and projectile range, discovering that a 45° launch angle yields the maximum range.

Research Brief:

  • The Siege of Constantinople
    • Task: Take on the role of an artillery commander to determine the correct launch angle for a cannon to hit the city walls.

Problem Set 3.2:

  • Angled Projectile Motion
    • Focus: More complex problems involving projectiles launched at various angles, requiring trigonometry to resolve initial velocity vectors into components.

Part 3: Relative Velocity and Synthesis

Lecture 3.3: Relative Velocity
Core Idea: Introduce the concept of relative velocity, the final major topic of the unit.

Hexagon Lab:

  • Hitting a Moving Target (Simulations)
    • Objective: Apply understanding of relative velocity to successfully hit a moving target in a simulation.

Problem Set 3.3:

  • Relative Velocity
    • Practice solving various relative velocity scenarios.

Flexbook Reading:

  • Review and Synthesis
    • Assign any remaining relevant sections from the Flexbook to help students review and solidify their understanding of all the unit’s concepts.

Unit 3 Review and Assessment

  • Comprehensive review of all Unit 3 concepts.
  • Unit test or culminating project assessing students’ ability to apply their knowledge of two-dimensional motion.
