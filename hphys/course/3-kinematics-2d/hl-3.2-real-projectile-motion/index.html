Unit 3 Phenomenon

The first effective use of large-scale artillery in the 1453 Siege of Constantinople, where success depended on moving from theory to practical, real-world application.

Part 1: Introduction

Engineer’s Challenge: The simulation provided a perfect world where every variable was known and the laws of physics worked without interference. On the battlefield, however, the real world is messy. The exact muzzle velocity of a cannon might be unknown, friction is always present, and precise measurements are difficult. An artillerist, like a modern engineer, must first test and characterize their equipment before they can hope to use it effectively.

Your Mission:

  • Treat your projectile launcher as an unknown system.
  • Design and execute experiments to determine its precise initial launch velocity ($v_i$).
  • Use that data to create a mathematical model that predicts, with measurable accuracy, where the projectile will land when fired at an angle.

Key Variables:

  • Initial velocity: $v_i$
  • Initial height: $\Delta y$
  • Horizontal range: $\Delta x$

Goal:

  • Fire the launcher perfectly horizontally from a known height to determine $v_i$.
  • Sketch and label your experimental setup, including all quantities to measure ($\Delta y$, $\Delta x$).
  • Write a clear procedure and prepare a data table for your measurements.
  • Use kinematic equations to calculate the projectile’s time in the air and its initial horizontal velocity.

Part 2: Investigation

(This lab is divided into a Pre-Lab Design phase and an Execution phase. Complete the design for both Parts A and B in your notebook before collecting any data.)

A. Characterizing the Launcher: Determining Muzzle Velocity

Goal: Determine the initial speed ($v_i$) of the projectile as it leaves the launcher. The most reliable way is to fire the launcher perfectly horizontally from a known height.

Pre-Lab Design Tasks:

  • Sketch: Draw a diagram of your experimental setup. Label all quantities to measure (e.g., initial height $\Delta y$, horizontal range $\Delta x$).
  • Procedure: Write a clear, step-by-step procedure. What tools will you use for each measurement? How many trials will you conduct for reliability?
  • Data Tables: Create a well-organized data table for your measurements.
  • Calculations: Outline the physics equations you will use. Starting from the kinematic equations, show how to calculate the projectile’s time in the air and use that to find its initial horizontal velocity.
  • Execution: Once approved, execute your experiment and record your data. Calculate the average initial velocity of your launcher.

B. Predicting the Range: The Angled Launch Test

Goal: Use the muzzle velocity from Part A to predict the landing spot of a projectile fired at a specific angle.

Pre-Lab Design Tasks:

  • Choose a launch angle between $30^\circ$ and $60^\circ$. State this angle clearly.
  • Using the average muzzle velocity from Part A and your chosen launch angle, mathematically predict the horizontal range ($\Delta x$) of the projectile. Show all calculations, resolving the initial velocity vector into $x$ and $y$ components.
  • Place a target (e.g., a circle on paper) at your predicted landing spot.
  • Execution: Fire the launcher. Measure the actual range and compare it to your prediction.

Part 3: Synthesis

Engineer’s Post-Mortem Report: In your notebook, analyze the results of your experiment.

  • Quantitative Analysis: Calculate the percent error between your predicted range and your actual measured range:
\[\%\ \text{Error} = \frac{|\text{Predicted Range} - \text{Actual Range}|}{\text{Predicted Range}} \times 100\%\]

  • Error Analysis: What are the most likely sources of discrepancy between your prediction and result? Be specific. (“Human error” is not sufficient—identify specific measurements or assumptions that could contribute.)

  • Model vs. Reality: The kinematic equations represent a “perfect” model of the physical world. Your experiment was a real-world test of that model. Based on your results, how well does the model describe reality in this case? What real-world factors (e.g., air resistance, friction within the launcher) does our simple physics model ignore?
