The Master Gunner’s Problem

Firing horizontally is simple. But to hit the walls of Constantinople, the gunners had to fire at an angle.

This creates a new challenge: The initial velocity ($v_i$) is no longer purely horizontal or vertical. It’s both.

Our entire strategy of separating motion into two worlds depends on finding the initial velocity in each direction.

The Secret: SOH CAH TOA

The secret to separating the velocities lies in simple trigonometry. The initial velocity vector is the hypotenuse of a right triangle. The horizontal and vertical velocities are the legs.

Horizontal (x-component): \(v_x = v_i \cos(\theta)\)

Vertical (y-component): \(v_{y,i} = v_i \sin(\theta)\)

These two equations are the only new tools you need for this entire topic.

The Physics is the Same

Once you use trigonometry to find the initial velocities, the physics is identical to what you already know.

The Horizontal Story: Constant Velocity

  • No forces (ignoring air resistance).
  • Velocity is constant: $v_x = v_i \cos(\theta)$
  • The only equation: $\Delta x = v_x t$

The Vertical Story: Constant Acceleration

  • Only gravity acts on the object.
  • Acceleration is constant: $a_g = -9.80\,\text{m/s}^2$
  • Use the kinematic equations with $v_{y,i} = v_i \sin(\theta)$

Worked Example: Breaching the Walls

A cannon fires a ball with an initial velocity of $150\,\text{m/s}$ at an angle of $35.0^\circ$ above the horizontal.

Question: What is the cannonball’s range ($\Delta x$)?

Step 1: Resolve the initial velocity vector.

  • $v_x = (150\,\text{m/s})\cos(35.0^\circ) = 123\,\text{m/s}$
  • $v_{y,i} = (150\,\text{m/s})\sin(35.0^\circ) = 86.0\,\text{m/s}$

Step 2: Find the total time of flight. (A vertical problem) Let’s find the time to the apex and double it. At the apex, $v_{y,f} = 0$.

\(v_{y,f} = v_{y,i} + a_g t\) \(0 = 86.0\,\text{m/s} + (-9.80\,\text{m/s}^2)t\) \(t_{apex} = \frac{-86.0\,\text{m/s}}{-9.80\,\text{m/s}^2} = 8.78\,\text{s}\) \(t_{total} = 2 \times t_{apex} = 17.6\,\text{s}\)

Step 3: Find the range. (A horizontal problem) \(\Delta x = v_x t_{total} = (123\,\text{m/s})(17.6\,\text{s}) = 2160\,\text{m}\)

Check for Understanding

A soccer ball is kicked with the same initial speed at three different angles: $20^\circ$, $45^\circ$, and $70^\circ$.

Which launch angle will have the largest initial vertical velocity ($v_{y,i}$)?

$70^\circ$, because $\sin(\theta)$ is largest for the largest angle.

Which launch angle will keep the ball in the air for the longest time?

$70^\circ$, because the largest initial vertical velocity means it takes longer to go up and come back down.

Which launch angle will have the largest initial horizontal velocity ($v_x$)?

$20^\circ$, because $\cos(\theta)$ is largest for the smallest angle.

Answering Our Essential Questions

1. How do we use trigonometry to find the initial horizontal and vertical velocities?

We model the initial velocity vector as the hypotenuse of a right triangle.

  • Horizontal Component: $v_x = v_i \cos(\theta)$
  • Vertical Component: $v_{y,i} = v_i \sin(\theta)$

2. What is the complete step-by-step process for solving angled projectile problems?

  • Step 1: Use trig to resolve $v_i$ into $v_x$ and $v_{y,i}$.
  • Step 2: Use the vertical motion kinematics to solve for time, $t$.
  • Step 3: Use the horizontal motion equation ($\Delta x = v_x t$) to solve for range (or vice-versa).
