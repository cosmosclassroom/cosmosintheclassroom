Chapter 4: The Rules of Constant Acceleration

Section 1 of 11
▶ Key Vocabulary (4 terms)
Constant Acceleration

A state of motion where the velocity changes by the same amount in every equal time interval, resulting in a constant slope on a velocity-time graph.

Kinematic Equations

A set of four algebraic equations that relate displacement, velocity, acceleration, and time for objects moving with constant acceleration.

Freefall

The motion of an object under the sole influence of gravity, where air resistance is negligible, and acceleration is constant ($\vec{a} = -\vec{a}_g$).

Terminal Velocity

The maximum velocity a falling object reaches when the force of air resistance equals the force of gravity, resulting in zero net force and zero acceleration.

\[\Delta x = v_i t + \frac{1}{2}at^2\]

\[\Delta x = \frac{1}{2}(v_i + v_f)t\]

Introduction: From Description to Prediction

In the previous chapter, we learned to analyze motion that has happened by using the visual language of graphs. We observed that the slope of a velocity-time graph tells us the acceleration. But what if we want to predict the trajectory of a rocket before it launches? We need a tool that moves beyond visual analysis—a set of elegant, powerful mathematical laws.

In the 17th century, scientists like Galileo noticed that many real-world motions, particularly the motion of falling objects, followed a simple rule: constant acceleration. This single observation allowed them to translate the dynamic story of motion into a handful of algebraic equations. These are the Kinematic Equations—a concise, perfect mathematical model that allows us to predict the future position and velocity of any object, provided its acceleration doesn’t change.

Phenomenon: Predicting the Apogee

Recall the sounding rocket: it accelerates violently during the engine burn, then it coasts. During the coasting phase (after engine burnout and before re-entry), the rocket is subjected almost exclusively to one dominant, constant force: gravity.

During this long, silent ascent to its apogee, the rocket’s acceleration is essentially a constant $\vec{a} = -9.80 \, \text{m/s}^2$ (or $-\vec{a}_g$). Engineers must use this known, constant value to predict the exact peak altitude (the apogee) and the exact moment vertical velocity becomes zero ($\vec{v} = 0$).

Consider This: If the rocket burns out at $350 \, \text{m/s}$ while traveling upwards, how much higher will it coast? Predicting this distance is the essence of this chapter. It requires using the constant acceleration model to solve for $\Delta x$.

The Four Kinematic Equations

The kinematic equations are derived from the definitions of constant acceleration and average velocity. Notice that each of the four equations is missing one of the five kinematic variables ($\Delta x, v_i, v_f, a, t$). This makes them a systematic tool: you only need three known variables to solve for any of the other two.

This equation comes directly from the definition of acceleration ($\vec{a} = \frac{\Delta \vec{v}}{\Delta t}$) and is useful when displacement is not relevant: \(v_f = v_i + at\)

This equation is derived by substituting the first equation into the average velocity definition, and it is the most commonly used: \(\Delta x = v_i t + \frac{1}{2}at^2\)

This is often the most powerful equation for problems like the rocket’s apogee, where the time to reach the destination is unknown and unneeded: \(v_f^2 = v_i^2 + 2a\Delta x\)

Special Case: Freefall

The most common application of constant acceleration is freefall. Near Earth’s surface, all objects, regardless of their mass, accelerate downward at the same rate, $\vec{g}$. \(\vec{a} = - \vec{g}\) where $\vec{g}$ is the vector representing the acceleration due to gravity, and its magnitude is $g = 9.80 \, \text{m/s}^2$.

When solving freefall problems:

  • Always Define Your Axis: Conventionally, ‘up’ is the positive ($\text{+y}$) direction, making the acceleration $\vec{a} = -9.80 \, \text{m/s}^2$.
  • The Apex Rule: At the very top of an object’s flight (its apogee or maximum height), its instantaneous vertical velocity is exactly $v_y = 0 \, \text{m/s}$.

Modeling Imperfection: The Role of Air Resistance

The kinematic equations are a perfect system model that assumes only constant acceleration. In reality, our sounding rocket is also subject to air resistance (or drag), which is not constant; it increases with the square of the object’s speed.

Air resistance acts as an opposing force, causing the net acceleration to be less than $9.80 \, \text{m/s}^2$ during the coasting ascent and less than $9.80 \, \text{m/s}^2$ during the fall. If an object falls far enough, the drag force eventually equals the gravitational force, resulting in zero net force and zero acceleration. At this point, the object stops accelerating and falls at a constant speed known as terminal velocity.

For the sounding rocket, air resistance significantly affects its landing spot, which is why engineers rely on more complex computer models rather than just the four simple kinematic equations.

Thinking Lens: Systems and System Models

The kinematic equations represent a powerful System Model. They show how five separate variables ($\Delta x, v_i, v_f, a, t$) are mathematically intertwined. This model is only valid if the system condition—constant acceleration—is met. When we analyze a rocket’s flight, we use the Kinematic Model for the coasting phase ($\vec{a} = -\vec{g}$) and a different, more complex model for the powered ascent (where $\vec{a}$ is changing). Understanding the System Model’s limitations is just as important as knowing the equations themselves.

The Bigger Picture: Synthesis and Connection

The four kinematic equations—simple as they are—are the cornerstone of classical physics. They allow us to precisely predict the motion of everything from a falling apple to a sounding rocket’s apogee. By creating this robust mathematical system, we are ready to ask the next, most profound question in physics: Why do things move and accelerate in the first place? That question leads us directly to the concepts of force and dynamics.
