Introduction: The Challenge of Aim

In the last chapter, we analyzed the simplest case of projectile motion: an object launched horizontally. But the world is rarely so straightforward. To throw a spear, launch a rocket, or fire a cannon at a distant target requires aim. It means launching the projectile upwards, into the sky, trusting that gravity will pull it back down in just the right place. This adds a new layer of complexity. The elegant arc of the projectile’s path now has both an upward and a downward journey. Our task in this chapter is to master this arc—not just to describe it, but to predict it with mathematical certainty.

Phenomenon: The Artillery Commander's Dilemma

Return to the walls of Constantinople in 1453. The immense power of Sultan Mehmed II’s cannons is useless without precision. The artillery commander faces a critical dilemma: at what angle should the barrel be aimed? Aim too low, and the massive stone ball will fall short, wasting precious gunpowder and time. Aim too high, and it may soar harmlessly over the walls, or take so long to arrive that its impact energy is reduced. The difference between victory and failure is a matter of degrees. To breach the walls, the commander must solve a two-dimensional physics problem where the initial velocity is no longer purely horizontal.

The Question: How can an artillery commander develop a model to predict the exact range of a cannonball based on its launch speed and angle?

The Rules of the Game: Deconstructing the Arc

Your investigation revealed a clear pattern. Now, we build the mathematical model to explain it. The core principle remains the same: the independence of motion. The horizontal and vertical stories are separate, linked only by time.

The only new step is that the initial velocity, $v_0$, is now directed at an angle, $\theta$. We cannot use this velocity directly. First, we must resolve it into its components using trigonometry:

  • The initial horizontal velocity is $v_{0x} = v_0 \cos(\theta)$. This value will never change throughout the flight (ignoring air resistance).
  • The initial vertical velocity is $v_{0y} = v_0 \sin(\theta)$. This value will immediately start to decrease due to gravity, become zero at the peak of the trajectory, and then become negative as the projectile falls.

Once we have these starting components, we can analyze the two stories just as before.

Example: The Angled Cannon

An artillery piece fires a cannonball with an initial speed of 120 m/s at an angle of 30.0° above the horizontal.

Part A: What are the initial horizontal and vertical components of the velocity?

Solution: $v_{0x} = v_0 \cos(\theta) = (120 \, \text{m/s}) \cos(30.0^\circ) = 104 \, \text{m/s}$ $v_{0y} = v_0 \sin(\theta) = (120 \, \text{m/s}) \sin(30.0^\circ) = 60.0 \, \text{m/s}$

Part B: How long is the cannonball in the air (total time of flight)?

Solution: This is a vertical problem. We can find the time it takes to reach the peak ($v_y = 0$). Knowns: $v_{0y} = 60.0 \, \text{m/s}$, $v_y = 0$, $a_g = -9.80 \, \text{m/s}^2$. Use $v_y = v_{0y} + a_g t$. $t_{peak} = \frac{v_y - v_{0y}}{a_g} = \frac{0 - 60.0 \, \text{m/s}}{-9.80 \, \text{m/s}^2} = 6.12 \, \text{s}$. The total time of flight is twice the time to the peak (assuming it lands at the same height). $t_{total} = 2 \times t_{peak} = 12.2 \, \text{s}$.

Part C: What is the range of the cannonball?

Solution: This is a horizontal problem using the total time of flight. Knowns: $v_x = 104 \, \text{m/s}$, $t = 12.2 \, \text{s}$. $d_x = v_x t = (104 \, \text{m/s})(12.2 \, \text{s}) = 1270 \, \text{m}$ (or 1.27 km).

Common Mistake: A very common mistake is to forget that the vertical velocity is zero at the highest point of the trajectory. This is a powerful piece of information that allows you to calculate the time to the peak and the maximum height.

Thinking Lens: Patterns

The universe is full of repeating patterns. The arc of a thrown ball, the path of a cannonball, and the trajectory of a water fountain jet all share the same mathematical shape: a parabola. This consistent pattern is not a coincidence. It is the visual result of two simple rules interacting over time: constant horizontal motion and constant vertical acceleration. By understanding the underlying rules, we gain predictive power over the pattern itself. Recognizing these patterns is a core practice of science, allowing us to see the simple, universal laws that govern seemingly complex events.

Synthesis: From Constantinople to the Cosmos

The ability to predict a trajectory changed the world. It gave the Ottoman Empire the key to a city that had been impenetrable for a millennium. But the intellectual framework born from this necessity did much more. This very same mathematics—separating motion into components, analyzing each part, and reassembling the whole—is what allows NASA engineers to plot a course for a probe to Mars. The probe is a projectile, launched at an angle, subject to the constant pull of the sun’s gravity. The scales are astronomically different, but the physics, the pattern, is the same. The quest to understand the flight of a cannonball gave humanity the tools to begin its journey to the stars.
