Introduction: The General Case

In the previous chapter, we solved the simplest case of projectile motion: the horizontal launch. But how does the Ottoman artillery commander aim his cannon to hit a wall hundreds of meters away? He can’t fire horizontally, or the cannonball would crash into the ground just outside his camp. He must fire at an angle.

Solving the general case of projectile motion requires combining two powerful tools we’ve already mastered: vector resolution (from Unit 1) and the independence of motion (from the previous chapter). By breaking the initial launch velocity ($\vec{v}_0$) into its horizontal and vertical components, we can solve the entire complex trajectory using two separate sets of 1D kinematic equations.

The First Step: Resolving the Vector

“When a projectile is launched at an angle ($\theta$) above the horizontal, the initial velocity ($\vec{v}_0$) is split between the horizontal and vertical dimensions.

  • Horizontal Component ($v_{0x}$): This component determines how fast the projectile travels across the ground. Since it is adjacent to the angle $\theta$, we use the cosine function. \(v_{0x} = v_0 \cos(\theta)\)
  • Vertical Component ($v_{0y}$): This component determines how high the projectile will fly and how long it stays in the air. Since it is opposite the angle $\theta$, we use the sine function. \(v_{0y} = v_0 \sin(\theta)\)

Once these two components are found, the initial velocity vector $\vec{v}_0$ is completely replaced by its two straight-line components. We then analyze the horizontal motion and the vertical motion separately, just as we did before.”

Discovery: Optimizing the Launch Angle

Discovery: Optimizing the Launch Angle

How does the angle affect the flight? A high angle gives you more time in the air but less horizontal speed. A low angle gives you more horizontal speed but less time in the air. This is a critical trade-off.

The Five-Step Strategy for Angled Projectiles

Solving any angled projectile problem is a systematic process. By following these five steps, you ensure you never mix up your independent variables.

  1. Resolve the Vector: Use trigonometry to find $v_{0x}$ and $v_{0y}$.
  2. Find Time of Flight (Vertical): Use a vertical kinematic equation to solve for the total time ($t$) the object spends in the air. Remember, the displacement $\Delta y$ for a projectile landing at the same height it was launched is zero, OR, you can solve for the time to the apex (where $v_y = 0$) and double it.
  3. Find the Range (Horizontal): Use the total time ($t$) found in Step 2 and the constant horizontal velocity ($v_{0x}$) in the horizontal motion equation. \(\Delta x = v_{0x} t\)
  4. Find Maximum Height (Vertical): Use the time to the apex and a vertical kinematic equation to solve for the maximum vertical displacement.
  5. Find Final Velocity (Vector Math): Use the final $v_x$ (which is $v_{0x}$) and the final $v_y$ to reassemble the final velocity vector ($\vec{v}_f$) using the Pythagorean theorem and the inverse tangent function.

Example: The Artillery Commander's Solution

Example: The Artillery Commander's Solution

An artillery commander aims a cannon with an initial velocity ($v_0$) of $150 \text{m/s}$ at a launch angle ($\theta$) of $35.0^\circ$.

Question A: What is the total time of flight before the cannonball returns to its launch height?

Step 1: Resolve the Vector \(v_{0y} = v_0 \sin(\theta) = (150 \text{m/s}) \sin(35.0^\circ) \approx 86.0 \text{m/s}\)

Step 2: Find Time of Flight (Using $\Delta y = 0$) Use $\Delta y = v_{0y} t + \frac{1}{2} a_y t^2$. Since $a_y = -9.80 \text{m/s}^2$ and $\Delta y = 0$: \(0 = (86.0 \text{m/s}) t + \frac{1}{2} (-9.80 \text{m/s}^2) t^2\) Solving for $t$ (excluding $t=0$): \(t = \frac{-2 \cdot v_{0y}}{a_y} = \frac{-2(86.0 \text{m/s})}{-9.80 \text{m/s}^2} \approx 17.55 \text{s}\)

Question B: What is the horizontal range?

Step 3: Find Horizontal Velocity \(v_{0x} = v_0 \cos(\theta) = (150 \text{m/s}) \cos(35.0^\circ) \approx 122.9 \text{m/s}\)

Step 4: Find Range \(\Delta x = v_{0x} t = (122.9 \text{m/s})(17.55 \text{s}) \approx 2157 \text{m}\)

Thinking Lens: Patterns

The lab on optimizing the launch angle revealed two profound Patterns:

  1. Maximum Range at $45^\circ$: When the launch angle is $45^\circ$, the horizontal and vertical components of the initial velocity ($v_{0x}$ and $v_{0y}$) are equal. This is the optimal trade-off: enough vertical velocity to stay airborne for a long time, and enough horizontal velocity to cover distance during that time.

  2. Complementary Angles: Any two angles that add up to $90^\circ$ (e.g., $30^\circ$ and $60^\circ$, or $20^\circ$ and $70^\circ$) will produce the exact same range. This symmetry reflects the underlying mathematical structure where the initial velocity is simply being redistributed between the sine and cosine functions. This predictable Pattern simplifies the job of the artillery commander immensely.

The Bigger Picture: From Cannons to Orbits

By mastering the component method, you’ve gained the ability to predict the path of any object thrown on Earth. The curved path of the cannonball is, mathematically, the same type of curve as the path of a stone thrown into a pond or a stream of water from a hose. This principle of analyzing motion by separating its dimensions is the final piece of the kinematics puzzle.
