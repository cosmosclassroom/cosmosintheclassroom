Introduction: Beyond the Straight Line

So far, our study of motion has been confined to a single dimension—objects moving forward and backward along a straight line. But the world is not so simple. A thrown baseball, a stream of water from a fountain, a leaping dolphin—all of these trace a curved path through space.

To understand this two-dimensional motion, we cannot use our 1D kinematic equations directly. We must first learn to see the world as a physicist does: as a system of independent motions, happening simultaneously.

Phenomenon: The Artillery Problem

For centuries, the success of armies depended on a deep, practical understanding of physics. An artillery crew needed to hit a target miles away, a target they often couldn’t see. Firing a cannonball was an act of prediction. If they fired it with too much upward angle, it would fall short. Too little angle, and it would fly too far or hit the ground too soon. The angle of launch and the initial speed from the gunpowder had to be calculated with precision. A small error in the initial vector could mean the difference between victory and defeat.

The Question: How can we use the principles of physics to create a mathematical model that accurately predicts the trajectory of a projectile, turning the art of aiming into a science?

The Rules of the Game: Independence of Motion

The single most important concept in all of projectile motion is the independence of horizontal and vertical motion. This means we can treat the two directions as entirely separate problems, connected only by the fact that they happen for the same amount of time.

Once a projectile is launched, gravity is the only force acting on it (we will ignore air resistance for now). Since gravity only pulls downward, it can only change the projectile’s vertical motion. It has absolutely no effect on its horizontal motion.

  • Horizontal (x-direction): The velocity is constant (\(v_x = \\text{constant}\)). There is no horizontal acceleration (\(a_x = 0\)).
  • Vertical (y-direction): The velocity changes due to gravity. There is a constant downward acceleration (\(a_y = -g = -9.8 \\, \\text{m/s}^2\)).

To analyze the motion, we must first use trigonometry to break the initial velocity vector, \(v_i\), into its x and y components.

Building the Model: A Two-Column Approach

The key to solving projectile motion problems is organization. Since the horizontal and vertical motions are independent, we can analyze them in separate columns.

Horizontal (x) Motion Vertical (y) Motion
\(a_x = 0\) \(a_y = -9.8 \\, \\text{m/s}^2\)
\(v_{ix} = v_i \\cos(\\theta)\) \(v_{iy} = v_i \\sin(\\theta)\)
\(\\Delta x = v_{ix} t\) \(\\Delta y = v_{iy} t + \\frac{1}{2}a_y t^2\)
  \(v_{fy} = v_{iy} + a_y t\)

The variable that links the two columns is time (\(t\)). We often solve for time in the vertical direction first and then use that time to find the horizontal range.

Example 2: Calculating Time of Flight and Range

For the cannonball in Example 1 (\(v_i = 150 \\, \\text{m/s}\) at \(35^{\\circ}\)), calculate its total time of flight and its range, assuming it lands at the same height it was launched from.

Part A: Time of Flight We’ll analyze the vertical motion. The cannonball lands when its vertical displacement (\(\\Delta y\)) is zero.

Knowns (y-direction):

  • \[v_{iy} = 86.0 \\, \\text{m/s}\]
  • \[a_y = -9.8 \\, \\text{m/s}^2\]
  • \[\\Delta y = 0 \\, \\text{m}\]

Use the displacement equation:

\[\\Delta y = v_{iy} t + \\frac{1}{2}a_y t^2\] \[0 = (86.0 \\, \\text{m/s})t + \\frac{1}{2}(-9.8 \\, \\text{m/s}^2)t^2\] \[0 = t(86.0 \\, \\text{m/s} - 4.9 \\, \\text{m/s}^2 \\cdot t)\]

This gives two solutions: \(t=0\) (the launch) and \(86.0 \\, \\text{m/s} - 4.9 \\, \\text{m/s}^2 \\cdot t = 0\), which gives \(t = \\frac{86.0 \\, \\text{m/s}}{4.9 \\, \\text{m/s}^2} = 17.6 \\, \\text{s}\).

The time of flight is 17.6 seconds.

Part B: Range Now we use the time of flight to analyze the horizontal motion.

Knowns (x-direction):

  • \[v_{ix} = 122.9 \\, \\text{m/s}\]
  • \[t = 17.6 \\, \\text{s}\]

Use the horizontal distance equation:

\[\\Delta x = v_{ix} t\] \[\\Delta x = (122.9 \\, \\text{m/s})(17.6 \\, \\text{s}) = 2163 \\, \\text{m}\]

The range of the cannonball is 2163 meters, or about 2.16 kilometers.

Common Mistake: Never mix x and y variables in the same equation (except for time). A vertical acceleration (\(a_y\)) can only be used with vertical velocities (\(v_{iy}, v_{fy}\)) and vertical displacement (\(\\Delta y\)).

Thinking Lens: Patterns in Motion

When you combine constant velocity in one direction with constant acceleration in another, a predictable and beautiful pattern emerges: the parabola. Every projectile, from a tossed key to a ballistic missile, follows this same essential shape. By understanding the simple, independent rules that govern each direction, we can predict the entire complex system. This is the power of a physical model. We’ve found the pattern that governs all motion in a gravitational field.
