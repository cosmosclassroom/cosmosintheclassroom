This special case, constant acceleration, is incredibly common and allows us to derive a set of powerful predictive equations. We don't need a rigorous calculus proof to understand where they come from; they are a logical consequence of the definitions we already know. If acceleration is constant, then the velocity changes at a steady rate. Our first equation is just a rearrangement of the definition of acceleration: **1. $v_1 = v_0 + at$** (Final velocity is the initial velocity plus the accumulated change). The distance an object travels is its average velocity multiplied by time. For constant acceleration, the average velocity is simply the average of the start and end velocities: **2. $d = \frac{v_0 + v_1}{2} t$** (Displacement is the average velocity times time). By substituting the first equation into the second, we can derive an equation that doesn't require us to know the final velocity: **3. $d = v_0 t + \frac{1}{2}at^2$** (Displacement is the initial velocity part plus the acceleration part). And finally, by combining them in a different way, we can get an equation that doesn't depend on time at all, which is incredibly useful: **4. $v_1^2 = v_0^2 + 2ad$** (The final velocity squared is the initial squared plus the contribution from acceleration over a distance). These four equations are the toolkit of kinematics. For any problem involving constant acceleration, if you know three of the five variables (d, v₀, v₁, a, t), you can find the other two. - type: image # A diagram showing an object being thrown upwards and falling back down. # On the way up, velocity vectors (upward) get shorter.
