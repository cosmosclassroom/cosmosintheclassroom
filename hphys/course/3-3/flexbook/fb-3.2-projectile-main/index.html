Chapter 6: Hitting the Target

Chapter 6: Hitting the Target

The Central Question

How do we adapt our model of projectile motion to predict the trajectory of objects launched at an angle, and how do we account for motion in a moving world?

The Narrative Arc

Having learned to see the hidden simplicity within a horizontally-launched projectile, our journey now turns to the more complex, more common, and more powerful case of angled launches. This is the chapter where we learn the rules not just for hitting the ground, but for precisely choosing our target. We will then expand our view to see that motion itself is relative, a crucial insight for navigating our dynamic world.

6.1 Introduction: The Archer’s Instinct

Long before the cannon, there was the archer. An expert archer does not aim directly at a distant target; they aim above it. Their experience has taught them an intuitive truth: the arrow will fall as it flies. To hit the mark, they must launch the projectile on a carefully chosen upward path, trading some forward motion for the precious gift of time in the air.

This chapter translates that archer’s instinct into the precise language of physics. We will add the power of trigonometry to our kinematic toolbox, allowing us to model any launch angle. Then, we will take one final step and ask: what if the archer is on a moving horse, or the target is on a moving ship? Welcome to the world of relative velocity.

6.2 The Formal Rules: Angled Projectiles

When a projectile is launched at an angle, its initial velocity ($v_i$) has both a horizontal and a vertical component. The key to solving these problems is to use trigonometry to break the initial velocity vector into its $x$ and $y$ pieces.

  • Initial Horizontal Velocity ($v_{x,i}$):
    This component is adjacent to the launch angle $\theta$. It remains constant throughout the flight.
    \(v_{x,i} = v_i \cos\theta\)

  • Initial Vertical Velocity ($v_{y,i}$):
    This component is opposite the launch angle $\theta$. It is affected by gravity and changes throughout the flight.
    \(v_{y,i} = v_i \sin\theta\)

Once we have these components, the problem becomes identical to the ones we’ve solved before: a horizontal story of constant velocity and a vertical story of constant acceleration.

Worked Example: The Trebuchet

A trebuchet launches a $100\,\text{kg}$ stone at an angle of $40.0^\circ$ with an initial speed of $50.0\,\text{m/s}$.

Question: What is the maximum height of the stone, and what is its total range?

Step 1: Resolve the initial velocity vector.

  • $v_{x,i} = (50.0\,\text{m/s})\cos(40.0^\circ) = 38.3\,\text{m/s}$
  • $v_{y,i} = (50.0\,\text{m/s})\sin(40.0^\circ) = 32.1\,\text{m/s}$

Step 2: Find the time to reach maximum height.
At the peak, $v_{y,f} = 0$.

Knowns:
$v_{y,i} = 32.1\,\text{m/s}$
$v_{y,f} = 0$
$a_g = -9.80\,\text{m/s}^2$

Use $v_{y,f} = v_{y,i} + a_g t$:

\[t = \frac{v_{y,f} - v_{y,i}}{a_g} = \frac{0 - 32.1\,\text{m/s}}{-9.80\,\text{m/s}^2} = 3.28\,\text{s}\]

Step 3: Calculate the maximum height ($\Delta y$).

Use $\Delta y = v_{y,i} t + \frac{1}{2} a_g t^2$:

\[\Delta y = (32.1\,\text{m/s})(3.28\,\text{s}) + \frac{1}{2}(-9.80\,\text{m/s}^2)(3.28\,\text{s})^2 = 52.7\,\text{m}\]

Step 4: Calculate the total time of flight.
Total time is twice the time to the peak:

\[t_\text{total} = 2 \times 3.28\,\text{s} = 6.56\,\text{s}\]

Step 5: Calculate the range ($\Delta x$).

\[\Delta x = v_{x,i} t_\text{total} = (38.3\,\text{m/s})(6.56\,\text{s}) = 251\,\text{m}\]

6.3 The Formal Rules: Relative Velocity

Motion often depends on your point of view. A ball tossed on a moving train looks simple to the person on the train, but its path looks like a parabola to an observer on the ground. We handle this with vector addition.

The rule:
The velocity of object A relative to frame C ($v_{AC}$) is the vector sum of A’s velocity relative to frame B ($v_{AB}$) plus B’s velocity relative to frame C ($v_{BC}$):

\[\vec{v}_{AC} = \vec{v}_{AB} + \vec{v}_{BC}\]

Worked Example: Boat Crossing a River

A boat that can travel $4.0\,\text{m/s}$ in still water ($v_{bw}$) tries to cross a river with a current of $3.0\,\text{m/s}$ ($v_{wg}$). What is the boat’s velocity relative to the ground ($v_{bg}$)?

We want $v_{bg}$. The formula is $v_{bg} = v_{bw} + v_{wg}$.

Since the boat’s velocity is perpendicular to the water’s velocity, use the Pythagorean theorem for the magnitude:

\[|v_{bg}| = \sqrt{(4.0\,\text{m/s})^2 + (3.0\,\text{m/s})^2} = 5.0\,\text{m/s}\]

The direction can be found with trigonometry:

\[\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{4.0}{3.0}\right) = 53^\circ \text{ upstream from straight across}\]

6.4 The Thinking Lens: Cause and Effect

The relationship between launch angle and range is a perfect example of Cause and Effect. The launch angle is the cause; the resulting trajectory (height and range) is the effect. By manipulating the equations, we can see these relationships clearly. An angle of $45^\circ$ gives the maximum range because it provides the optimal balance between the horizontal velocity component (which moves the projectile forward) and the vertical velocity component (which determines the time of flight). Changing the cause (the angle) produces a predictable change in the effect (the range). Understanding these relationships is the key to all engineering and design.

6.5 The Bigger Picture: From Cannonballs to Cosmos

The rules you’ve learned in this unit are truly universal. The same mathematics that describes a cannonball’s arc also describes the path of a comet swinging around the sun. The principles of relative velocity are essential for everything from air traffic control—where a plane’s velocity relative to the ground is the sum of its velocity relative to the air plus the wind’s velocity—to the calculations that allow GPS satellites to provide accurate positions to a moving car. By breaking complex motion into simple, manageable components, we unlocked a predictive power that has enabled much of modern technology and exploration.
