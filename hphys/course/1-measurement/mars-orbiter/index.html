Question 1 (5 points)

Segment 1: The Root of the Error The Mars Climate Orbiter was lost because one engineering team used English units (pounds) while another used metric units (Newtons). The thrusters on the orbiter were designed to produce a force measured in Newtons (N). The software, however, calculated the required force in pound-force (lbf).

Convert a force of 1.5 lbf to its equivalent in Newtons.

N

Use the conversion factor 1 lbf ≈ 4.45 N. Calculation: 1.5 lbf * 4.45 N/lbf = 6.675 N.

Question 2 (5 points)

If the software thought it was requesting a tiny thruster firing of 0.5 N, but the hardware interpreted this as 0.5 lbf, how much actual force was produced in Newtons?

N

The hardware produced 0.5 lbf of force. Convert this to Newtons: 0.5 lbf * 4.45 N/lbf = 2.225 N.

Question 3 (10 points)

Synthesis (Cause and Effect): The difference between the intended force (0.5 N) and the actual force (2.225 N) seems small. How can such a tiny, persistent error (cause) lead to the complete loss of a $125 million spacecraft (effect)?

Question 4 (5 points)

Metacognition: When you perform a unit conversion, how do you check that your answer makes sense? For example, should the number of Newtons be larger or smaller than the number of pounds-force? Why?

Question 5 (5 points)

Segment 2: Practice with Navigational Units The Mars Climate Orbiter was traveling at approximately 5.5 km/s as it approached the planet. Convert this speed to miles per hour (mi/h).

mi/h

(5.5 km/s) * (0.621 mi/km) * (3600 s/h) ≈ 12295.8 mi/h

Question 6 (5 points)

The planned altitude for the orbiter’s closest approach was 150 km. The actual trajectory brought it to 57 km. What is the difference in altitude in feet (ft)?

ft

Difference is 93 km. (93 km) * (1000 m/km) * (3.28 ft/m) = 305,040 ft.

Question 7 (10 points)

Synthesis (Scale, Proportion, and Quantity): A NASA report stated the navigation error was about 4.45 N⋅s per day. This is a measure of impulse (Force × time). How does this calculation demonstrate that small, seemingly insignificant quantities can become critically important when scaled up over a long duration or distance?

Question 8 (5 points)

Metacognition: The problems in this segment involved multiple conversion steps. What strategy did you use to keep your work organized and ensure you didn’t miss a step?

Question 9 (5 points)

Segment 3: Deconstructing a Trajectory Vector Imagine the orbiter is firing its main engine, creating a thrust vector of 500 N at an angle of 20.0° relative to its direction of travel. Calculate the forward component of the thrust (Fx). This is the part of the force that changes the spacecraft’s forward speed.

N

Fx = F * cos(θ) = 500 N * cos(20.0°) ≈ 469.8 N

Question 10 (5 points)

Using the same scenario from the previous question, calculate the sideways component of the thrust (Fy). This is the part of the force that pushes the spacecraft off its intended course.

N

Fy = F * sin(θ) = 500 N * sin(20.0°) ≈ 171.0 N

Question 11 (5 points)

Synthesis (Cause and Effect): Using the vector components you just calculated, explain how a single engine firing (the cause) can produce two different outcomes simultaneously (changing forward speed and moving sideways, the effects).

Question 12 (5 points)

Metacognition: In your own words, why is it useful to break a single vector, like thrust, into perpendicular components? How does this simplify the problem?

Question 13 (5 points)

Segment 4: The Quantitative & Qualitative Critique You are given the following summary data:

  • Planned closest altitude: 150,000 m
  • Actual closest altitude: 57,000 m
  • Minimum survivable altitude: 80,000 m
  • Root Cause: Software produced impulse values in lbf⋅s instead of the required N⋅s.

Quantitative: Calculate the percentage error between the planned and actual altitudes.

%
(150000 - 57000) / 150000 * 100% = 62%
Question 14 (5 points)

Quantitative: Using the same data, calculate how far below the minimum survivable altitude the orbiter actually flew, expressed in meters.

m

80,000 m - 57,000 m = 23,000 m
