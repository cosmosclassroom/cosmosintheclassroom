Chapter 2 Assessment: The Story of a Sounding Rocket

Anchoring Phenomenon: The multi-stage launch and trajectory of a suborbital sounding rocket.

Instructions

  • Read each problem carefully.
  • For all calculations, you must begin with the relevant starting formula, rearrange it algebraically to solve for the unknown variable, and then substitute your known values.
  • All work must be shown, and final answers must include the correct SI units and be rounded to the proper number of significant figures.
  • You must state a consistent coordinate system (e.g., “up is the positive direction”) for your solutions.
  • Assume the acceleration due to gravity is $a_g = 9.80 \, \text{m/s}^2$ and that air resistance is negligible.

Segment 1 of 3: The Apprentice’s Telemetry (Core Skills)

A. Context & Phenomenon Link

Telemetry is the stream of data rockets send back to Earth. Your first task is to analyze the most basic data from the initial engine burn to ensure your fundamental kinematic tools are calibrated.

B. Core Practice

  1. A sounding rocket accelerates uniformly from rest on the launch pad. After $4.50 \, \text{s}$, its velocity is $125 \, \text{m/s}$ upward. What is the rocket’s average acceleration during this “first stage” burn?

  2. What is the rocket’s altitude at the end of these $4.50 \, \text{s}$?

  3. Using the acceleration you calculated in question 5, what would the rocket’s final velocity be if the engine continued to burn until it reached an altitude of 500. m?

C. Metacognitive Prompt

When you chose a “positive” direction for your coordinate system, how did that choice affect the signs of your known values (like acceleration) and your final answers?

Segment 2 of 3: The Journeyman’s Flight Path (Synthesis & Application)

A. Context & Phenomenon Link

After the first engine burns out, the rocket “coasts” upward, slowing down under the influence of gravity alone. Your task is to model this second, unpowered stage of its flight.

B. Core Practice

A different rocket has an engine that accelerates it to a velocity of $185 \, \text{m/s}$ at an altitude of $2.20 \, \text{km}$. At this exact moment, the engine cuts out.

This graph shows the rocket’s complete trajectory from launch to landing.

Time on this graph is measured from the instant the engine cuts out.

  1. From the moment the engine cuts out, how much additional time does the rocket take to reach its maximum altitude (its “apogee”)?

  2. How much additional altitude does the rocket gain during its coasting phase (from engine cutoff to apogee)?

  3. Sketch two simple graphs for the “coasting” portion of the flight only: a position-time graph ($y$ vs. $t$) and a velocity-time graph ($v$ vs. $t$). Be sure to label your axes and show the correct shape of the curves/lines.

C. Cross-Cutting Lens (Cause and Effect)

During the coasting phase, the rocket’s velocity and acceleration have opposite signs. Explain how this mathematical relationship (the cause) produces the physical effect of the rocket slowing down as it rises.

Segment 3 of 3: The Master’s Analysis (Complex Scenarios)

A. Context & Phenomenon Link

The final challenge is to handle a scenario with multiple moving objects, proving you can model and compare two distinct trajectories to predict a future event.

B. Core Practice: The Intercept Problem

Rocket A is launched from rest with a constant upward acceleration of $15.0 \, \text{m/s}^2$. Exactly $3.00 \, \text{s}$ later, Rocket B is launched from the same pad with a constant upward acceleration of $30.0 \, \text{m/s}^2$.

  1. Write two distinct equations for the vertical position ($\Delta y$) as a function of time for Rocket A ($\Delta y_A$) and Rocket B ($\Delta y_B$). (Hint: For Rocket B, its time of travel will be $t - 3.00 \, \text{s}$, where $t$ is the time since Rocket A launched).

  2. At what time after Rocket A’s launch does Rocket B overtake Rocket A?

  3. What is their altitude when this occurs?

C. Qualitative Critique & Extension

In the problem you just solved, we assumed the rockets could accelerate forever. In reality, a sounding rocket’s engine burns out at a certain altitude, and it begins to coast. Describe, in words, how the problem would change if Rocket A’s engine cut out at $t = 8.00 \, \text{s}$. Would Rocket B still be able to catch it? What different calculations would you need to perform to find out for sure? (You do not need to do the calculations).
