Mapping World

1.6 A New Tool: Describing Direction So far, we have learned how to answer the question, “How much?” But in our quest to map the world, this is only half the story. To give directions, you need to know more than just how far to walk; you need to know which way to walk.

Science makes a crucial distinction between quantities that are just a magnitude (like temperature or distance) and those that have both a magnitude and a direction. The first kind are called scalars. The second, more powerful kind are called vectors. A distance of 5 kilometers is a scalar. A displacement of 5 kilometers to the northeast is a vector. To truly map our world, we need the language of vectors.

1.8 The Rules of the Game Part II: The Grammar of Vectors The simulation in your lab showed that we can add vectors by placing them “head-to-tail.” While this is a useful visual, it can be imprecise. To achieve the accuracy needed for scientific work, we use a more powerful method that breaks a vector down into its fundamental components.

Just as any location on a map can be described by its east-west (x-coordinate) and north-south (y-coordinate) position, any vector can be described by its x- and y-components. By using simple trigonometry (SOH CAH TOA), we can convert a vector from its magnitude-and-direction form (e.g. 5 km at 30° North of East) into its component form.

The power of this method is that it turns a complex geometry problem into simple arithmetic. To add two vectors, you simply add their x-components together to get the new x-component, and add their y-components together to get the new y-component. This is the mathematical engine that allows a GPS to take multiple signals from different directions and calculate your single, precise location.

A Note on Navigation Bearings

Before solving the next problem, it is important to understand a specific angle convention used in navigation. While standard mathematical angles are measured counter-clockwise from the positive x-axis (East), a bearing is an angle measured clockwise from the North direction.

To correctly calculate the x and y components for a vector given as a bearing, you must first convert it into a standard angle.

North corresponds to a standard angle of 90° East corresponds to a standard angle of 0° South corresponds to a standard angle of 270° West corresponds to a standard angle of 180°

Tip: Always draw a quick coordinate system to visualize the bearing and find its equivalent standard angle before using \(\sin(\theta)\) or \(\cos(\theta)\) in your calculations.
