Unit 1 Research Brief: The Forecast

Speaking the Same Language

Before uncovering the great laws of the universe, scientists must agree on a shared, universal language to describe it.

Scenario

Imagine you are an ancient cartographer tasked with creating the first reliable map of a newly discovered area—our classroom. You have only rudimentary tools (string, protractor, stride, measuring stick). Your map must be clear enough for a stranger to navigate and find a hidden object.

Central Question

How can you create a reliable map of the classroom using only rudimentary tools, and what rules must you establish to ensure your map is understandable to anyone?

Your Task: The Forecast

This is the first stage of your Research Brief. Your goal is to create a detailed plan (“forecast”) for the challenges you will face and the procedures you will use.

Address the following points:

Your Toolkit

  • What specific, rudimentary tools will you use? Why did you choose them?

Your Procedure

  • Describe the step-by-step process you will follow. Where will you start?
  • What key features of the room will you measure?
  • How will you measure both distances and angles?

The Rules of the Map

  • Scale: How will you represent a large distance in the room on a small piece of paper? (e.g., “1 of my steps = 1 cm on the map”)
  • Origin & Direction: Where is your starting point (0,0)? How will you indicate direction (North, South, East, West)?
  • Key/Legend: What symbols will you use for important objects (desks, doors, windows)?

Anticipating Uncertainty

  • What are the biggest potential sources of error or uncertainty in your measurements?
  • How will you try to minimize them?

Thinking Lens: Scale, Proportion, and Quantity

  • How does this challenge force you to think about the relationship between a small-scale measurement (like the length of a desk) and a larger-scale representation (your map)?
  • Why is establishing a clear proportion (your scale) the most critical step in making a map that is scientifically honest?

Unit 1 Research Brief: The Blueprint

Speaking the Same Language

Objective

Refine your initial plan for mapping the classroom by incorporating vectors.

From Forecast to Blueprint

In the “Forecast” stage, you planned how to map the classroom. Now, use vectors—quantities with both magnitude and direction—to create a more precise plan.

Your Task: Create a Vector-Based Measurement Plan

Your goal: Write a plan so clear and detailed that another group could follow it exactly and produce a nearly identical map.

Blueprint Template

1. The Refined Research Question:
How can we use displacement vectors to determine the precise location of key objects in our classroom relative to a single, chosen origin?

2. The Coordinate System:

  • Origin: Where will the (0,0) point of your map be? (e.g., “The front-left corner of the teacher’s desk”). Why did you choose this point?
  • Axes: Which way is the positive x-direction (East)? Which way is the positive y-direction (North)? Include a simple sketch.

3. The Measurement Procedure:

  • Key Objects: List at least five key objects in the classroom you will map.
  • Magnitude (Distance): What tool will you use to measure the straight-line distance from your origin to each object? How will you ensure this measurement is as accurate as possible?
  • Direction (Angle): What tool will you use to measure the angle of each object’s displacement vector relative to your positive x-axis? How will you ensure this measurement is precise?

The Ultimate Test: Mars Rover Landing

Landing a robotic rover on Mars is a challenge of incredible complexity, but at its heart, it is a problem of speaking the same language.

A Cautionary Tale

In 1999, NASA’s Mars Climate Orbiter was lost because one engineering team used imperial units (pounds of force) while the spacecraft’s software expected metric units (Newtons). This small discrepancy compounded over time, causing the orbiter to enter the Martian atmosphere at the wrong angle and be lost forever. This real-world example shows that for science and engineering, a shared language is essential.

The Language of Success

For successful rover landings, every measurement had to be perfect. Teams on Earth and the probe millions of miles away had to use the exact same, unambiguous physical language. This included units, velocity, atmospheric density, gravitational pull, timing, and more. Honesty about uncertainty was also critical—calculating margins of error and planning for worst-case scenarios.

Conclusion

The ability to explore other worlds, build global networks, and advance knowledge rests on the foundation of agreeing to speak the same language. The rovers on Mars are monuments to this principle.
