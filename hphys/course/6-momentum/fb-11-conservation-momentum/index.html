\ How can we analyze interactions and collisions using a principle where a\n
\ key quantity is always conserved?\n anchoringPhenomenon: >-\n In
\ the late 1970s, humanity launched two of its most ambitious explorers, the Voyager
\ probes, on a grand tour of the outer solar system. To make the journey possible,
\ engineers relied on a celestial trick\u2014a ‘gravity assist’ or ‘slingshot’
\ maneuver. By flying breathtakingly close to massive planets like Jupiter, the
\ tiny spacecraft were able to ‘steal’ a tiny fraction of the planet’s orbital
\ energy to dramatically increase their own speed, flinging them towards their
\ next destination. This seemingly magical boost, a cornerstone of interplanetary
\ travel, is not magic at all; it is a profound demonstration of one of physics’
\ most powerful and unwavering laws: the conservation of momentum.\n keyVocabulary:\n
\ - term: Momentum\n definition: >-\n The product of the
\ mass and velocity of an object; a vector\n quantity often described
\ as ‘mass in motion’. It is a measure of an object’s resistance to being stopped.\n
\ - term: Impulse\n definition: >-\n The change in momentum
\ of an object when the object is acted upon by\n a force for a certain
\ interval of time. A large force for a short time can produce the same impulse
\ as a small force for a long time.\n - term: Elastic Collision\n
\ definition: >-\n A collision in which there is no net loss in kinetic
\ energy in the\n system as a result of the collision. The objects bounce
\ off each other perfectly, like billiard balls.\n - term: Inelastic Collision\n
\ definition: >-\n A collision in which kinetic energy is not
\ conserved, typically being transformed into other forms like heat or sound.
\ A ‘perfectly inelastic’ collision is one where the objects stick together after
\ impact.\n keyFormulae: |\n - $p = mv$\n - $J = F\Delta t = \
Delta p$\n - $\Sigma p_0 = \Sigma p_1$\n - $m_1v_{1,0} + m_2v_{2,0}

\ flexbook content\ninstructional_flow:\n - type: reading\n title: \Introduction
\ to Momentum"\n content: |\n When we analyze the motion of objects in
\ the universe, we often focus on forces and energy. But there’s another powerful
\ way to understand interactions between objects: through the concept of momentum
\ and its conservation. Momentum\u2014defined as the product of mass and velocity\u2014
gives us insights into everything from car crashes to the Voyager mission’s gravity
\ assists.\n\n - type: reading\n title: "Defining Momentum"\n content:
\ |\n Momentum is a vector quantity defined as the product of an object’s
\ mass and velocity: $p = mv$. This quantity measures how difficult it is to stop
\ a moving object. A massive truck moving slowly can have the same momentum as
\ a small car moving quickly. The direction of momentum is the same as the direction
\ of velocity.\n\n - type: activity_prompt\n title: "Momentum Comparison
\ Exercise"\n content: |\n Calculate and compare the momentum of different
\ objects:\n 1. A 1500 kg car traveling at 25 m/s\n 2. A 0.145 kg baseball
\ thrown at 40 m/s\n 3. A 75,000 kg freight train car moving at 10 m/s\n
\ \n Which has the greatest momentum? How much greater is it than the
\ smallest momentum?\n\n - type: reading\n title: "The Impulse-Momentum Theorem"
\n content: |\n When a force acts on an object over a period of time,
\ it changes the object’s momentum. This change is called impulse, defined as
\ $J = F\Delta t = \Delta p$. The impulse-momentum theorem states that the impulse
\ applied to an object equals its change in momentum.\n \n This relationship
\ explains why airbags work: they extend the time over which a force acts, reducing
\ the average force experienced during a collision while still providing the same
\ impulse needed to stop a person’s momentum.”
