  • unitNumber: 6 unitTitle: Voyager’s Gravity Assist unitSubtitle: The Conservation of Momentum centralQuestion: >- How can we analyze interactions and collisions using a principle where a key quantity is always conserved? anchoringPhenomenon: >- In the late 1970s, humanity launched two of its most ambitious explorers, the Voyager probes, on a grand tour of the outer solar system. To make the journey possible, engineers relied on a celestial trick—a ‘gravity assist’ or ‘slingshot’ maneuver. By flying breathtakingly close to massive planets like Jupiter, the tiny spacecraft were able to ‘steal’ a tiny fraction of the planet’s orbital energy to dramatically increase their own speed, flinging them towards their next destination. This seemingly magical boost, a cornerstone of interplanetary travel, is not magic at all; it is a profound demonstration of one of physics’ most powerful and unwavering laws: the conservation of momentum. keyVocabulary:
    • term: Momentum definition: >- The product of the mass and velocity of an object; a vector quantity often described as ‘mass in motion’. It is a measure of an object’s resistance to being stopped.
    • term: Impulse definition: >- The change in momentum of an object when the object is acted upon by a force for a certain interval of time. A large force for a short time can produce the same impulse as a small force for a long time.
    • term: Elastic Collision definition: >- A collision in which there is no net loss in kinetic energy in the system as a result of the collision. The objects bounce off each other perfectly, like billiard balls.
    • term: Inelastic Collision definition: >- A collision in which kinetic energy is not conserved, typically being transformed into other forms like heat or sound. A ‘perfectly inelastic’ collision is one where the objects stick together after impact. keyFormulae: |
    • $p = mv$
    • $J = F\Delta t = \Delta p$
    • $\Sigma p_0 = \Sigma p_1$

instructional_flow:

  • type: reading title: “Introduction to Momentum” content: | When we analyze the motion of objects in the universe, we often focus on forces and energy. But there’s another powerful way to understand interactions between objects: through the concept of momentum and its conservation. Momentum—defined as the product of mass and velocity—gives us insights into everything from car crashes to the Voyager mission’s gravity assists.

  • type: reading title: “Defining Momentum” content: | Momentum is a vector quantity defined as the product of an object’s mass and velocity: $p = mv$. This quantity measures how difficult it is to stop a moving object. A massive truck moving slowly can have the same momentum as a small car moving quickly. The direction of momentum is the same as the direction of velocity.

  • type: activity_prompt title: “Momentum Comparison Exercise” content: | Calculate and compare the momentum of different objects:
    1. A 1500 kg car traveling at 25 m/s
    2. A 0.145 kg baseball thrown at 40 m/s
    3. A 75,000 kg freight train car moving at 10 m/s

    Which has the greatest momentum? How much greater is it than the smallest momentum?

  • type: reading title: “The Impulse-Momentum Theorem” content: | When a force acts on an object over a period of time, it changes the object’s momentum. This change is called impulse, defined as $J = F\Delta t = \Delta p$. The impulse-momentum theorem states that the impulse applied to an object equals its change in momentum.

    This relationship explains why airbags work: they extend the time over which a force acts, reducing the average force experienced during a collision while still providing the same impulse needed to stop a person’s momentum.
