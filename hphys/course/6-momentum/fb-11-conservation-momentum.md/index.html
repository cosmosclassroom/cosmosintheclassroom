To make the journey possible, engineers relied on a celestial trick—a 'gravity assist' or 'slingshot' maneuver. This seemingly magical boost, a cornerstone of interplanetary travel, is not magic at all; it is a profound demonstration of one of physics' most powerful and unwavering laws: the conservation of momentum. keyVocabulary: - term: Momentum definition: >- The product of the mass and velocity of an object; a vector quantity often described as 'mass in motion'. It is a measure of an object's resistance to being stopped. - term: Impulse definition: >- The change in momentum of an object when the object is acted upon by a force for a certain interval of time. A large force for a short time can produce the same impulse as a small force for a long time. - term: Elastic Collision definition: >- A collision in which there is no net loss in kinetic energy in the system as a result of the collision. The objects bounce off each other perfectly, like billiard balls. - term: Inelastic Collision definition: >- A collision in which kinetic energy is not conserved, typically being transformed into other forms like heat or sound. This quantity measures how difficult it is to stop a moving object. A massive truck moving slowly can have the same momentum as a small car moving quickly. The direction of momentum is the same as the direction of velocity. - type: activity_prompt title: "Momentum Comparison Exercise" content: | Calculate and compare the momentum of different objects: 1. This change is called impulse, defined as $J = F\Delta t = \Delta p$. The impulse-momentum theorem states that the impulse applied to an object equals its change in momentum. This relationship explains why airbags work: they extend the time over which a force acts, reducing the average force experienced during a collision while still providing the same impulse needed to stop a person's momentum.
