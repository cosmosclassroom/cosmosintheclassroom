Unit 2 Assessment: Sounding Rocket Kinematic Analysis

Anchoring Phenomenon: The flight of the “Aether-1,” a suborbital sounding rocket designed to collect atmospheric data. Your task is to analyze the telemetry data and develop a complete kinematic model of its vertical flight, from launch to impact.

Core Concepts: Uniform Acceleration, Freefall, Graphical Analysis, Kinematic Equations.

Standard Values: Use $ \vec{a}_g = 9.80 \text{ m/s}^2$ for the magnitude of acceleration due to gravity. Assume the rocket launches from rest at ground level ($\vec{x}_i = 0.0 \text{ m}$). Ignore the effects of air resistance for all calculations unless otherwise noted.

Segment 1 of 4: The Launch Phase (Powered Ascent) (Estimated Time: 15 minutes)

A. Context & Phenomenon Link

The Aether-1 mission begins with the main engine firing. For this initial phase, the rocket experiences a large, constant upward acceleration as it powers through the lower atmosphere.

B. Core Practice

The rocket’s engine provides a constant upward acceleration of $\vec{a} = +50.0 \text{ m/s}^2$. The engine burns for a duration of $12.0 \text{ s}$ before shutting down (burnout).

  1. Calculate the velocity ($\vec{v}_f$) and altitude ($\Delta\vec{x}$) of the rocket at the moment of engine burnout.
  2. On separate, clearly labeled axes, sketch the acceleration-time ($\vec{a}$ vs. $t$), velocity-time ($\vec{v}$ vs. $t$), and position-time ($\vec{x}$ vs. $t$) graphs for this $12.0 \text{ s}$ powered ascent phase only.

C. Synthesis & Cross-Cutting Lens (Scale, Proportion, and Quantity)

Quantitatively compare the rocket’s acceleration during launch to the acceleration due to gravity ($\vec{a}_g$). What does this ratio tell you about the magnitude of the engine’s thrust force compared to the gravitational force acting on the rocket?

D. Metacognitive Prompt

What simplifying assumption did we make about the rocket’s acceleration during the engine burn? Why is this assumption likely not perfectly accurate for a real rocket?

Segment 2 of 4: The Coasting Phase (Ballistic Flight) (Estimated Time: 15 minutes)

A. Context & Phenomenon Link

At burnout, the engine cuts off and the rocket is now in freefall, even as it continues to travel upward. It coasts silently, losing speed as gravity pulls it back toward Earth, until it reaches its maximum altitude (apogee). The initial conditions for this segment are the final conditions from Segment 1.

B. Core Practice

Use the velocity and altitude at burnout from Segment 1 as your initial conditions for this phase.

  1. Calculate the additional time it takes for the rocket to reach its apogee after engine burnout.
  2. Calculate the additional altitude the rocket gains during this coasting phase.
  3. Determine the rocket’s absolute maximum altitude (its apogee) above the ground.

C. Synthesis & Cross-Cutting Lens (Patterns)

Describe the pattern of the rocket’s motion during its coast to apogee. If you were to continue the velocity-time graph from Segment 1, what would the slope of the line in this new segment represent, both numerically and physically?

D. Metacognitive Prompt

When beginning your analysis of this segment, why is it critical to establish a new set of initial conditions ($t_i, \vec{x}_i, \vec{v}_i$) corresponding to the moment of engine burnout?

Segment 3 of 4: The Descent (Estimated Time: 13 minutes)

A. Context & Phenomenon Link

Having reached its apogee, the rocket begins its long fall back to Earth. For this analysis, we will model its entire descent as uninterrupted freefall, ignoring parachute deployment.

B. Core Practice

Use the apogee altitude from Segment 2 as your initial condition for this phase. The rocket begins its descent from rest.

  1. Calculate the total time it takes for the rocket to fall from apogee back to the ground.
  2. Calculate the final velocity, $\vec{v}_f$, of the rocket the instant before it impacts the ground.
  3. Is the time for the rocket to travel from launch to apogee greater than, less than, or equal to the time it takes to fall from apogee to the ground? Justify your answer.

C. Synthesis & Cross-Cutting Lens (Cause and Effect)

The primary cause of the rocket’s acceleration during its entire coasting phase (both up and down) is gravity. How does this single, constant cause produce the different effects of the rocket slowing down on its way up and speeding up on its way down?

D. Metacognitive Prompt

What is the physical significance of the negative sign in your answer for the final velocity in problem B.2? Why would an answer of simply $|\vec{v}_f|$ be incomplete?

Segment 4 of 4: Metacognition & Synthesis (Estimated Time: 10 minutes)

A. Context & Phenomenon Link

The mission analysis is nearly complete. The final task is to combine the data from all phases of flight into a single, comprehensive model that tells the full story of the Aether-1’s journey.

B. Core Practice

  1. Synthesis: On a single set of axes, sketch a complete, qualitatively accurate velocity-time graph for the Aether-1’s entire flight, from launch ($t=0$) to impact. Label the key events on the time-axis: (a) Engine Burnout, (b) Apogee, and (c) Impact.
  2. Transfer: Another rocket, the “Aether-2,” is designed to reach the same apogee as the Aether-1. However, the Aether-2’s engine provides a smaller average acceleration. To reach the same apogee, must the Aether-2’s engine burn for a longer, shorter, or equal amount of time compared to the Aether-1? Justify your reasoning.

C. Synthesis & Cross-Cutting Lens (Systems and System Models)

Our entire analysis has been a system model of the rocket’s flight. This model intentionally ignored the effect of air resistance. How would including air resistance in our model affect the actual apogee and the actual impact velocity compared to the values you calculated?

D. Metacognitive Prompt

Reflect on your problem-solving process. For which part of the rocket’s flight (ascent, coast, or descent) was a graphical representation most helpful for your understanding? For which part was a purely algebraic approach more efficient? Explain why.
